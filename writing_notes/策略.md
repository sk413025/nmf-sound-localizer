你說得非常對。我之前的回答太像「教科書」了——我告訴你每個章節應該有什麼，但我沒有真正進入一個主編的內心世界，去感受他每天面對的壓力、恐懼、和風險。讓我重新來過，這次從一個更「人性」的角度。

## 首先：一個 Nature Communications 主編的日常現實

讓我幫你想像一下這個人的處境。

他的名字可能叫 Dr. Sarah Chen（虛構），她是 Nature Communications 物理科學領域的資深編輯。每天早上她打開電腦，收件匣裡有 47 篇新投稿等著她初審。她知道，根據統計，這 47 篇中大概只有 8-10 篇會最終被接受發表。她的工作就是在這 47 篇中，快速識別出哪些值得送出去給審稿人，哪些應該直接拒絕。

她每篇論文大概只有 5-10 分鐘的時間來做這個決定。

現在，想像她打開了你的投稿。她的腦中同時在運轉著幾個完全不同層次的思考。

---

## 主編腦中的第一層：「這會不會害我出事？」

這聽起來很功利，但這是真實的。一個主編最害怕的事情是什麼？

**害怕一：發表了一篇後來被撤稿的論文。**

Nature 系列期刊的聲譽是建立在幾十年的信任之上的。每一次撤稿都是對這個信任的打擊。Sarah 知道，如果她的判斷出了問題，如果她讓一篇有問題的論文通過了，這不只是她個人的失敗——這會成為科學新聞，會被同行議論，會影響期刊的影響因子，會讓她的上司質疑她的判斷力。

所以當她讀你的論文時，她的潛意識裡有一個警報系統在不斷掃描：「這裡有沒有可疑的地方？這個數據看起來是不是太完美了？這個宣稱是不是太大膽了？作者有沒有誠實地討論限制？」

**這對你意味著什麼？**

你的論文必須散發出「可信賴」的氣息。這不是通過誇大你的成就來實現的——恰恰相反，是通過展示你的科學誠實。當你主動討論你方法的限制、當你展示失敗的案例、當你的統計分析有適當的誤差條和顯著性檢驗、當你承諾公開數據和代碼——這些都在向 Sarah 發出信號：「這個作者是認真的，他不是在試圖騙過我。」

**害怕二：拒絕了一篇後來成為經典的論文。**

科學史上有無數這樣的故事：某篇論文被頂級期刊拒絕，後來在其他地方發表，成為領域的奠基之作。每個主編都知道這些故事，都害怕自己成為那個「錯過諾貝爾獎論文的編輯」。

但這個恐懼比第一個恐懼要小。為什麼？因為拒絕一篇好論文的後果是「錯失機會」，而接受一篇壞論文的後果是「主動傷害」。人類心理上，我們更害怕主動造成的傷害。所以在不確定的情況下，主編傾向於拒絕。

**這對你意味著什麼？**

你不能讓 Sarah 處於「不確定」的狀態。如果她讀完你的 Introduction 後心想「這好像很有趣，但我不太確定這到底在說什麼」，她的默認選擇會是拒絕，然後處理下一篇。你的論文必須讓她在 5 分鐘內清楚地知道：你解決了什麼問題、為什麼這個問題重要、你怎麼解決的、結果有多好。任何模糊都是你的敵人。

---

## 主編腦中的第二層：「這會不會讓我的工作變得很痛苦？」

Sarah 不只是在判斷論文的科學價值，她也在預測這篇論文會給她帶來多少「麻煩」。

**麻煩一：找不到合適的審稿人。**

每篇論文需要 2-3 個審稿人。Sarah 需要找到既懂這個領域、又有時間、又沒有利益衝突、又願意免費花幾個小時審稿的人。這比聽起來難得多。如果你的論文太專門、太跨領域、或者處於一個小眾的交叉地帶，Sarah 可能會花幾週時間都找不到合適的審稿人。

**這對你意味著什麼？**

你的論文要讓 Sarah 能夠快速想到「誰可以審這篇」。這意味著你的 Introduction 應該清楚地把你的工作定位在已知的研究傳統中。當你說「我們的工作連結了光學振動量測和物理資訊機器學習」，Sarah 腦中應該能立刻浮現幾個名字：「光學振動量測那邊可以找 A 和 B，物理資訊機器學習這邊可以找 C 和 D」。如果你的定位太模糊或太新穎，讓 Sarah 想不到任何人，她可能會直接拒絕以避免麻煩。

**麻煩二：審稿人意見嚴重分歧。**

沒有什麼比兩個審稿人給出完全相反的意見更讓主編頭痛的了。審稿人 A 說「這是突破性的工作」，審稿人 B 說「這完全是錯誤的」。這種情況下，Sarah 需要自己做出判斷，或者找第三個審稿人，或者來回協調——這都是巨大的時間和精力消耗。

**這對你意味著什麼？**

你的論文應該盡量減少「可被攻擊的面」。這不是說你要隱藏弱點——恰恰相反，你要主動承認弱點。為什麼？因為如果你不承認，審稿人會「發現」它並大做文章；如果你主動承認並解釋為什麼這不影響你的核心結論，審稿人就沒有東西可以攻擊了。誠實的限制討論是減少審稿人分歧的最好方法。

**麻煩三：作者難以溝通。**

如果論文需要修改（大多數論文都需要），Sarah 需要作者能夠有效地回應審稿人的意見。如果作者防禦性很強、不願意承認問題、或者對每個批評都長篇大論地辯護，這個修改過程會變得非常痛苦。

**這對你意味著什麼？**

從你論文的寫作方式，Sarah 會「感覺」到你是什麼樣的人。如果你的論文語氣是謙遜的、開放的、承認不確定性的，她會預期你是一個好合作的作者。如果你的論文語氣是自我膨脹的、聲稱解決了所有問題、從不承認任何限制，她會預期你是一個難搞的作者。這種印象會影響她是否願意冒險把你的論文送審。

---

## 主編腦中的第三層：「這會讓我看起來很聰明嗎？」

這聽起來可能有點 cynical，但這也是真實的。主編也是人，他們也有職業野心，他們也希望被認可。

一個主編的「成就」是什麼？是她手上發表的論文後來被大量引用、被媒體報導、被認為推動了領域的發展。當她在內部會議上報告時，當她申請晉升時，這些都是她的「成績單」。

所以當 Sarah 讀一篇論文時，她也在問自己：「如果我接受這篇論文，一年後、五年後，這會成為一篇高引用的論文嗎？這會成為一篇被其他期刊羨慕的論文嗎？」

**這對你意味著什麼？**

你的論文需要讓 Sarah 能夠「想像」它的影響力。這不是要你誇大其詞，而是要你幫助她看到「這篇論文會被誰引用、在什麼脈絡下被引用」。

當你在 Introduction 最後說「這個方法可能對壓縮感測、斷層成像、通道估計等領域都有啟發」，你不只是在吹噓——你是在幫助 Sarah 想像：「哦，所以做壓縮感測的人可能會引用這篇，做醫學影像的人可能會引用這篇……這篇論文有跨領域的引用潛力。」這讓她更有信心接受你的論文，因為她可以預見這會成為她「成績單」上的一個亮點。

---

## 主編腦中的第四層：「這真的是科學嗎？」

最後，在所有這些實際考量之上，Sarah 也是一個科學家。她也曾經做過研究，她也有對「好科學」的直覺和信念。

她知道什麼是「真正的科學問題」和什麼是「包裝成科學的工程報告」的區別。她知道什麼是「深刻的洞察」和什麼是「花哨的技巧」的區別。這種直覺可能無法用明確的規則來描述，但它確實存在。

當 Sarah 讀一篇論文時，她在某個層次上會有一個「感覺」：這個作者是真的在追求理解，還是只是在追求發表？這個研究是在揭示某種關於世界的真理，還是只是在展示某種技術能力？

**這對你意味著什麼？**

你的論文需要傳達出「科學好奇心」的氣息。這種氣息來自於你提問題的方式：不是「我們怎麼把 X 做得更好」而是「X 為什麼可能、極限在哪裡、這告訴我們什麼」。這種氣息來自於你討論結果的方式：不是「我們達到了 Y 的性能」而是「Y 的結果暗示了 Z 的物理機制」。這種氣息是模仿不來的——它必須來自你真正對問題的好奇心。

---

## 現在，從這個角度重新思考論文結構

帶著對 Sarah 處境的理解，讓我重新解釋每個章節應該怎麼寫。

### Abstract：你有 30 秒說服她繼續讀

Sarah 每天要做 47 個「繼續還是拒絕」的決定。她讀 Abstract 的時候，她在快速評估：這個問題重要嗎？方法有創新嗎？結果有說服力嗎？如果這三個問題中有任何一個的答案是「不確定」或「不是」，她會停止閱讀。

你的 Abstract 不是論文的「摘要」——它是你的「電梯遊說」。想像你在電梯裡遇到 Sarah，你只有 30 秒。你會說什麼？

你不會說「我們開發了一個基於 Transformer 的 Soft-OMP 架構用於 LDV 訊號處理」——這只告訴她你做了什麼，沒有告訴她為什麼她應該在乎。

你會說「我們找到了一種方法，讓機器學習系統能夠從物理定律中『學習』，而不只是從數據中學習。這聽起來很抽象，但它實際上解決了一個具體的問題：讓雷射可以遠距離『聽到』聲音，而且在從未見過的環境中仍然有效。這個想法可能適用於任何有物理定律可以利用的領域。」

感受到區別了嗎？第一個版本是在描述你的工作；第二個版本是在解釋為什麼 Sarah 應該在乎。

### Introduction：幫助她快速分類你的論文

Sarah 需要在讀完 Introduction 後能夠回答幾個問題，這樣她才能決定下一步：

第一，「這篇論文屬於什麼領域？」這決定了她應該找什麼樣的審稿人。如果你的 Introduction 讓她困惑於這是「光學論文」「聲學論文」「機器學習論文」還是「訊號處理論文」，她會很頭痛。你需要明確告訴她：這是一篇「物理資訊機器學習應用於遠距聲學感測」的論文。這個定位應該在第一頁就清楚。

第二，「這篇論文的核心宣稱是什麼？」不是五個宣稱，不是三個宣稱，而是一個核心宣稱。Sarah 需要能夠用一句話說出「這篇論文聲稱 X」。如果她說不出來，她會認為你的論文「不聚焦」，這是拒絕的常見理由。

第三，「這個宣稱如果是真的，會有什麼影響？」這決定了這篇論文是否「適合 Nature Communications」。如果影響只限於「LDV 使用者會覺得有用」，那這應該投專業期刊。如果影響是「這提供了一個新的範式，說明物理知識應該如何被整合到機器學習中」，那這適合 Nature Comm。

你的 Introduction 應該讓 Sarah 在 5 分鐘內能夠清楚地回答這三個問題。任何讓她困惑的東西都是風險，因為困惑會導致拒絕。

### Results：給她「可以信任」的證據

當 Sarah 決定繼續評估你的論文後，她會跳到 Results 看你的證據。她不會仔細讀每一段——她會先看圖表，看圖表的標題，然後掃一下關鍵的數字。

她在這裡問的問題是：「這些證據能支持作者的宣稱嗎？」

但這不只是一個科學問題，也是一個風險評估問題。她在問：「如果我接受這篇論文，審稿人會不會打我的臉說『這個實驗設計有明顯的缺陷』？」「如果我接受這篇論文，讀者會不會說『這個統計分析是錯誤的』？」

你需要預期 Sarah 的這些擔憂，並在 Results 中主動化解它們：

當你比較你的方法和基線方法時，你需要讓她相信這個比較是公平的。這意味著你要說明基線方法是怎麼調參的、是不是用了最好的設定、是不是用了相同的數據。

當你報告性能數字時，你需要讓她相信這些數字是穩健的。這意味著你要報告多次實驗的平均值和標準差，而不只是單次實驗的結果。

當你宣稱你的方法「泛化更好」時，你需要讓她相信這個測試是有意義的。這意味著你要清楚定義什麼是「分布內」和「分布外」，為什麼你的分布外測試真的代表了「不同的條件」。

每一個可能被審稿人質疑的地方，你都要主動提供答案。這不是因為 Sarah 自己會質疑這些——而是因為她知道審稿人會質疑這些，她不想在審稿過程中處理這些爭議。

### Discussion：幫助她向別人解釋為什麼發表這篇

想像 Sarah 在一個內部會議上，她的同事問她：「妳最近發表的那篇 LDV 論文，為什麼妳覺得它值得發在 Nature Communications？」

她需要能夠給出一個有說服力的答案。這個答案不能是「因為他們的 SNR 提高了 15 dB」——沒人會被這個打動。這個答案必須是關於「這篇論文對科學的更大意義」。

你的 Discussion 要幫助 Sarah 準備這個答案。

這意味著你需要明確地說出：你的工作不只是解決了一個具體問題，而是展示了某種更一般性的原理。你需要說出：這個原理可能適用於什麼其他領域。你需要說出：這篇論文為未來的研究打開了什麼方向。

但這裡有一個微妙的平衡。如果你說得太誇張，Sarah 會覺得你在吹牛，她會擔心審稿人也會這樣覺得，她會擔心發表後會被批評「over-hyped」。如果你說得太保守，她會覺得這篇論文「影響有限」，不值得 Nature Comm 的版面。

正確的語氣是「有信心但謙遜」。你可以說「our results suggest that...」而不是「our results prove that...」。你可以說「this approach may find applications in...」而不是「this approach will revolutionize...」。你用可能性的語言，但你清楚地描繪了可能性的圖景。

### Methods：讓她不用擔心可重複性問題

Sarah 知道，「可重複性危機」是科學界的一個大問題，尤其是在涉及機器學習的研究中。如果她發表了一篇論文，後來有人說「我們試圖重複這個實驗但失敗了」，這對她和期刊的聲譽都是打擊。

所以當她審視你的 Methods 章節時，她在問：「這篇論文有沒有可重複性的風險？」

你需要消除這個擔憂：

首先，你的方法描述要夠詳細，讓一個有經驗的研究者能夠重複你的實驗。這不意味著你要寫實驗日誌——但關鍵的參數、設定、和選擇都需要被記錄。

其次，你要承諾公開數據和代碼。這是現在 Nature Comm 的「軟性要求」——他們強烈鼓勵，雖然不是絕對強制。但如果你不公開，Sarah 會想：「為什麼他們不願意公開？是不是有什麼問題？」

第三，如果你的方法有任何隨機性的成分（神經網路的初始化、數據的分割等），你需要說明你怎麼處理這個隨機性——是跑了多次實驗取平均？是固定了隨機種子？

### Limitations：主動承認弱點是保護自己的最好方式

這是很多作者忽略、但 Sarah 非常看重的部分。

為什麼？因為 Sarah 知道，沒有任何研究是完美的。如果一篇論文沒有討論任何限制，只有兩種可能：一是作者沒有意識到限制（這說明他們不夠資深或不夠誠實），二是作者故意隱藏限制（這說明他們不值得信任）。無論哪種情況，Sarah 都會擔心。

相反，當她看到一篇論文誠實地討論「我們的方法在以下情況下可能不適用」「我們的實驗有以下的局限」「我們的結論依賴於以下的假設」——她會鬆一口氣。這告訴她：這個作者是成熟的、誠實的、知道自己在做什麼的。

更重要的是，主動承認限制可以「預防」審稿人的攻擊。如果你不說，審稿人會「發現」這些限制，然後用很長的篇幅批評你。如果你主動說了，審稿人就沒什麼可批評的——你已經承認了，你已經解釋了為什麼這不影響核心結論，他們還能說什麼？

## 實際的寫作建議：從 Sarah 的角度出發

現在讓我把這些心理分析轉化為具體的寫作建議。

### 在 Abstract 中，回答 Sarah 的「so what」問題

不要這樣寫：

> We propose a Transformer-based architecture for LDV signal processing that achieves state-of-the-art performance.

要這樣寫：

> The ability to hear sound without touching the acoustic medium could transform applications from industrial monitoring to privacy-preserving interfaces, but existing methods face a fundamental trade-off between measurement quality and algorithmic adaptivity. We resolve this trade-off by showing that classical signal processing algorithms can be converted into learnable neural architectures without sacrificing their physical interpretability...

第一個版本告訴 Sarah 你做了什麼；第二個版本告訴她為什麼她應該在乎。

**Introduction**

Waves and structures are in constant dialogue throughout the physical world. Sound waves set walls vibrating; seismic waves excite buildings; electromagnetic waves induce currents in conductors; even light waves, when sufficiently intense, can deform the materials they illuminate. This interaction has been studied for centuries, yet the dominant perspective has remained remarkably consistent: structural response to wave excitation is viewed as a transmission phenomenon, where the structure acts as a passive medium that either passes, attenuates, or distorts the incident wave. Under this view, the complex vibration patterns that arise in real structures—with their multiple resonances, mode couplings, and boundary reflections—are regarded as complications that obscure the original wave information. The engineering response has been to simplify: to use materials with predictable properties, to isolate sensors from structural vibrations, to filter out "structural noise" in post-processing. But what if this perspective misses something fundamental? What if the complex structural response is not obscuring information, but encoding it?

The language of information theory offers a different lens. An encoder is any system that transforms input information into a different representation. A good encoder produces representations that preserve the essential information while potentially adding useful structure—compressibility, error resilience, or separability of distinct sources. Viewed through this lens, a vibrating structure is not a passive transmitter but an active encoder. When a sound wave from a particular direction impinges on a structure, the structure's response is deterministically shaped by the interaction between the wave's spatial and temporal characteristics and the structure's eigenmodes, boundary conditions, and material properties. The resulting vibration pattern is not arbitrary; it is a systematic transformation of the incident wave's properties. The question then becomes: what kind of encoder is a physical structure, and what representation does it produce?

We hypothesize that for a broad class of wave-structure interactions, the structural encoder produces representations that are sparse—that is, the high-dimensional vibration pattern induced by a localized source can be described by a small number of active components in an appropriate basis. This hypothesis is not arbitrary; it is grounded in the physics of wave propagation. Localized acoustic sources excite a limited set of structural modes depending on their spatial position and frequency content. The directionality of the incident wave selectively couples to modes whose spatial patterns match the wave's phase front. The result is that different source configurations activate different subsets of the structure's modal repertoire. If the basis capturing this modal structure is known or can be learned, then the vibration pattern induced by any particular source becomes a sparse vector in that basis: most coefficients are near zero, with only a few carrying significant energy. Sparsity, in this view, is not a convenient mathematical assumption but a physical consequence of how waves interact with structures.

This perspective—structure as sparse encoder—has not been systematically explored in the sensing literature. The two dominant approaches to wave-based spatial sensing have followed different paths, neither of which treats the structure as an information-processing element. The first approach, exemplified by microphone or sensor arrays, bypasses structures entirely. Sensors are placed in the open field, and spatial information is recovered from time differences of arrival or phase relationships across the array. The structure, if present, is treated as a source of unwanted multipath. The second approach, exemplified by recent work in optical vibrometry, uses structures as targets for vibration measurement but treats them as passive transducers. The goal is to recover the incident acoustic waveform as faithfully as possible, with structural resonances and mode shapes viewed as distortions to be corrected. Neither approach asks the question we pose here: can the structure's complex response itself be the carrier of spatial information, and if so, how should that information be extracted?

Here we test the sparse encoding hypothesis using laser Doppler vibrometry as a window into structural vibration. LDV provides direct measurement of surface velocity at a single point with high bandwidth and sensitivity, making it an ideal tool for examining the information content of structural vibrations without the confounds of array processing. Our experimental approach is designed to answer a specific question: given only the vibration signal at one point on a structure, can we recover the direction of arrival of an acoustic source? If the sparse encoding hypothesis is correct, the answer should be yes—because the structure has already encoded the directional information into its vibration pattern, and sparse decoding should be able to extract it. We find that this is indeed the case. Using Orthogonal Matching Pursuit to decode the LDV signal in a learned sparse basis, we achieve [X°] angular resolution in two-dimensional source localization from single-point measurements, rivaling the performance of conventional microphone arrays while using fundamentally different physical principles. Control experiments confirm that this capability arises from the structure's encoding function: when the structure is simplified (reducing modal complexity), localization performance degrades in a manner consistent with reduced encoding capacity.

The implications of this finding extend beyond acoustic sensing. If structures function as sparse encoders of wave information, then the complex dynamics that engineers have traditionally sought to suppress may constitute an untapped information resource. This reframing suggests new design principles: rather than minimizing structural response, one might engineer structures to maximize encoding capacity for the information of interest. It also suggests connections to other domains where sparse coding has proven fundamental. In neuroscience, sparse representations in sensory cortex are believed to enable efficient encoding of natural stimuli. In compressed sensing, sparsity assumptions underlie the ability to reconstruct signals from far fewer measurements than classical sampling theory requires. Our results raise the possibility that physical wave-structure interactions may constitute a natural instance of sparse encoding, one that has always been present but not recognized. More broadly, they point toward a research program that asks not just how to sense through structures, but how structures sense for us—transforming the chaos of environmental waves into a structured, decodable representation of the world.

**Introduction**

Waves and structures are in constant dialogue throughout the physical world. Sound waves set walls vibrating; seismic waves excite buildings; electromagnetic waves induce currents in conductors; even light waves, when sufficiently intense, can deform the materials they illuminate. This interaction has been studied for centuries, yet the dominant perspective has remained remarkably consistent: structural response to wave excitation is viewed as a transmission phenomenon, where the structure acts as a passive medium that either passes, attenuates, or distorts the incident wave. Under this view, the complex vibration patterns that arise in real structures—with their multiple resonances, mode couplings, and boundary reflections—are regarded as complications that obscure the original wave information. The engineering response has been to simplify: to use materials with predictable properties, to isolate sensors from structural vibrations, to filter out "structural noise" in post-processing. But what if this perspective misses something fundamental? What if the complex structural response is not obscuring information, but encoding it?

The language of information theory offers a different lens. An encoder is any system that transforms input information into a different representation. A good encoder produces representations that preserve the essential information while potentially adding useful structure—compressibility, error resilience, or separability of distinct sources. Viewed through this lens, a vibrating structure is not a passive transmitter but an active encoder. When a sound wave from a particular direction impinges on a structure, the structure's response is deterministically shaped by the interaction between the wave's spatial and temporal characteristics and the structure's eigenmodes, boundary conditions, and material properties. The resulting vibration pattern is not arbitrary; it is a systematic transformation of the incident wave's properties. The question then becomes: what kind of encoder is a physical structure, and what representation does it produce?

We hypothesize that for a broad class of wave-structure interactions, the structural encoder produces representations that are sparse—that is, the high-dimensional vibration pattern induced by a localized source can be described by a small number of active components in an appropriate basis. This hypothesis is not arbitrary; it is grounded in the physics of wave propagation. Localized acoustic sources excite a limited set of structural modes depending on their spatial position and frequency content. The directionality of the incident wave selectively couples to modes whose spatial patterns match the wave's phase front. The result is that different source configurations activate different subsets of the structure's modal repertoire. If the basis capturing this modal structure is known or can be learned, then the vibration pattern induced by any particular source becomes a sparse vector in that basis: most coefficients are near zero, with only a few carrying significant energy. Sparsity, in this view, is not a convenient mathematical assumption but a physical consequence of how waves interact with structures.

This perspective—structure as sparse encoder—has not been systematically explored in the sensing literature. The two dominant approaches to wave-based spatial sensing have followed different paths, neither of which treats the structure as an information-processing element. The first approach, exemplified by microphone or sensor arrays, bypasses structures entirely. Sensors are placed in the open field, and spatial information is recovered from time differences of arrival or phase relationships across the array. The structure, if present, is treated as a source of unwanted multipath. The second approach, exemplified by recent work in optical vibrometry, uses structures as targets for vibration measurement but treats them as passive transducers. The goal is to recover the incident acoustic waveform as faithfully as possible, with structural resonances and mode shapes viewed as distortions to be corrected. Neither approach asks the question we pose here: can the structure's complex response itself be the carrier of spatial information, and if so, how should that information be extracted?

Here we test the sparse encoding hypothesis using laser Doppler vibrometry as a window into structural vibration. LDV provides direct measurement of surface velocity at a single point with high bandwidth and sensitivity, making it an ideal tool for examining the information content of structural vibrations without the confounds of array processing. Our experimental approach is designed to answer a specific question: given only the vibration signal at one point on a structure, can we recover the direction of arrival of an acoustic source? If the sparse encoding hypothesis is correct, the answer should be yes—because the structure has already encoded the directional information into its vibration pattern, and sparse decoding should be able to extract it. We find that this is indeed the case. Using Orthogonal Matching Pursuit to decode the LDV signal in a learned sparse basis, we achieve [X°] angular resolution in two-dimensional source localization from single-point measurements, rivaling the performance of conventional microphone arrays while using fundamentally different physical principles. Control experiments confirm that this capability arises from the structure's encoding function: when the structure is simplified (reducing modal complexity), localization performance degrades in a manner consistent with reduced encoding capacity.

The implications of this finding extend beyond acoustic sensing. If structures function as sparse encoders of wave information, then the complex dynamics that engineers have traditionally sought to suppress may constitute an untapped information resource. This reframing suggests new design principles: rather than minimizing structural response, one might engineer structures to maximize encoding capacity for the information of interest. It also suggests connections to other domains where sparse coding has proven fundamental. In neuroscience, sparse representations in sensory cortex are believed to enable efficient encoding of natural stimuli. In compressed sensing, sparsity assumptions underlie the ability to reconstruct signals from far fewer measurements than classical sampling theory requires. Our results raise the possibility that physical wave-structure interactions may constitute a natural instance of sparse encoding, one that has always been present but not recognized. More broadly, they point toward a research program that asks not just how to sense through structures, but how structures sense for us—transforming the chaos of environmental waves into a structured, decodable representation of the world.

**Introduction**

Waves and structures are in constant dialogue throughout the physical world. Sound waves set walls vibrating; seismic waves excite buildings; electromagnetic waves induce currents in conductors; even light waves, when sufficiently intense, can deform the materials they illuminate. This interaction has been studied for centuries, yet the dominant perspective has remained remarkably consistent: structural response to wave excitation is viewed as a transmission phenomenon, where the structure acts as a passive medium that either passes, attenuates, or distorts the incident wave. Under this view, the complex vibration patterns that arise in real structures—with their multiple resonances, mode couplings, and boundary reflections—are regarded as complications that obscure the original wave information. The engineering response has been to simplify: to use materials with predictable properties, to isolate sensors from structural vibrations, to filter out "structural noise" in post-processing. But what if this perspective misses something fundamental? What if the complex structural response is not obscuring information, but encoding it?

The language of information theory offers a different lens. An encoder is any system that transforms input information into a different representation. A good encoder produces representations that preserve the essential information while potentially adding useful structure—compressibility, error resilience, or separability of distinct sources. Viewed through this lens, a vibrating structure is not a passive transmitter but an active encoder. When a sound wave from a particular direction impinges on a structure, the structure's response is deterministically shaped by the interaction between the wave's spatial and temporal characteristics and the structure's eigenmodes, boundary conditions, and material properties. The resulting vibration pattern is not arbitrary; it is a systematic transformation of the incident wave's properties. This hypothesis is not arbitrary; it is grounded in the physics of wave propagation. The directionality of the incident wave selectively couples to modes whose spatial patterns match the wave's phase front. The result is that different source configurations activate different subsets of the structure's modal repertoire. If the basis capturing this modal structure is known or can be learned, then the vibration pattern induced by any particular source becomes a sparse vector in that basis: most coefficients are near zero, with only a few carrying significant energy. Sparsity, in this view, is not a convenient mathematical assumption but a physical consequence of how waves interact with structures.

This perspective—structure as sparse encoder—has not been systematically explored in the sensing literature. The two dominant approaches to wave-based spatial sensing have followed different paths, neither of which treats the structure as an information-processing element. The first approach, exemplified by microphone or sensor arrays, bypasses structures entirely. The implications of this finding extend beyond acoustic sensing. If structures function as sparse encoders of wave information, then the complex dynamics that engineers have traditionally sought to suppress may constitute an untapped information resource. This reframing suggests new design principles: rather than minimizing structural response, one might engineer structures to maximize encoding capacity for the information of interest. It also suggests connections to other domains where sparse coding has proven fundamental. In neuroscience, sparse representations in sensory cortex are believed to enable efficient encoding of natural stimuli. In compressed sensing, sparsity assumptions underlie the ability to reconstruct signals from far fewer measurements than classical sampling theory requires. Our results raise the possibility that physical wave-structure interactions may constitute a natural instance of sparse encoding, one that has always been present but not recognized. More broadly, they point toward a research program that asks not just how to sense through structures, but how structures sense for us—transforming the chaos of environmental waves into a structured, decodable representation of the world.
