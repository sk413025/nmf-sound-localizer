# Non-contact acoustic sensing via the natural physical encoding of everyday objects

## Abstract
Conventional acoustic direction sensing relies on microphone arrays, limiting miniaturization and deployment in harsh or constrained environments. Here we show that everyday objects can themselves act as direction-dependent physical encoders: incident sound excites dispersive structural vibrations whose superposition produces a single-point spectral fingerprint measurable by a non-contact laser Doppler vibrometer. Modeling the angle-frequency response as a response matrix reveals a rapidly decaying singular-value spectrum, indicating a limited number of dominant encoding channels. This low-rank structure motivates a sparse inverse formulation over a calibrated dictionary of angle-specific spectral templates. To solve this inverse problem, we use a physics-guided neural solver with learned routing while preserving consistency with the physical dictionary. Under held-out speech evaluation on a 37-angle grid spanning 0-180° with 5° resolution, the solver sustains robust decoding down to 0 dB signal-to-noise ratio, and its learned routing structure aligns with the physical angle manifold, providing an interpretable link between data-driven inference and the underlying modal physics. Across five everyday targets, we reproduce direction-dependent fingerprints and maintain lower direction-of-arrival error than an analytical sparse-recovery baseline based on orthogonal matching pursuit under per-object calibration and retraining.

## Introduction
Extracting spatial information from wave fields is a recurring challenge spanning acoustics, optics, and seismology. In real environments, structural dispersion, multiple scattering, and boundary reverberation entangle propagation paths and produce seemingly chaotic time-domain signals [@rotter2017complex_media; @kuttruff2025room_acoustics]. Classical direction-of-arrival (DOA) estimation therefore relies on spatial sampling with sensor arrays and analytical inversion, including beamforming [@capon1969fkw], subspace methods such as MUSIC and ESPRIT [@schmidt1986music; @roy1989esprit], and microphone-array processing techniques reviewed in [@krim1996array; @vantrees2002optimum; @johnson1993array; @brandstein2001microphone_arrays; @benesty2008microphone_array]. However, the need for an aperture imposes severe constraints on size and placement, limiting integration in wearable platforms, structural monitoring systems, and compact devices deployed in harsh or space-constrained environments [@grumiaux2022_ssl_survey_deep_learning]. These constraints motivate a direction-sensing mechanism that does not depend on a spatial aperture.

Single-sensor alternatives suggest that such a mechanism may exist, but their physical basis remains unresolved. El Badawy and Dokmanić demonstrated that a single microphone embedded in an ad hoc LEGO scatterer can localize sound sources by exploiting a direction-dependent transfer function [@elbadawy2018lego_doa]. More recent studies show that a single structural vibration measurement can carry directional cues because acoustic incidence excites structure-borne waves whose response depends on incidence direction [@dipassio2022_audio_capture_structural_sensors; @dipassio2023_waspaa_wake_word; @dipassio2023doa_single_sensor; @rutowski2024reverb_single_sensor; @rutowski2025_structural_beamforming]. In parallel, sparse reconstruction offers an alternative DOA viewpoint by discretizing an angular manifold and recovering a sparse angular spectrum from compressed measurements [@malioutov2005sparse_doa; @donoho2006compressed_sensing; @candes2006robust_uncertainty; @candes2008compressive_sampling]. Convex relaxations such as basis pursuit and \(\ell_1\)-regularized regression provide classical baselines [@chen2001basis_pursuit; @tibshirani1996lasso], while greedy pursuit algorithms including matching pursuit and orthogonal matching pursuit (OMP) offer efficient solvers that retain interpretability [@mallat1993matching_pursuits; @pati1993omp; @tropp2007omp]. Array-free alternatives also exist in the form of acoustic vector sensors, which infer DOA from collocated pressure-particle-velocity measurements but require specialized instrumentation [@nehorai1994vector_sensor]. What remains unclear is whether these directional cues reflect a reproducible, physically compressible encoding mechanism that can be calibrated systematically across distinct everyday targets rather than target-specific idiosyncrasies.

To test this possibility, we recast everyday objects as physical encoders that convert acoustic incidence direction into a direction-dependent superposition of dispersive structural vibrations and, in turn, into a single-point spectral fingerprint. This perspective is inspired by computational wave physics, where complexity can be harnessed rather than suppressed, as in time-reversed acoustics [@fink1997time; @draeger1997one], single-pixel imaging [@duarte2008single], structural health monitoring [@ing2008lamb], transmission-matrix and wavefront-shaping views of complex media [@popoff2010transmission_matrix; @mosk2012complex_media; @rotter2017complex_media], and randomized metamaterials designed for compressive vibration identification [@jiang2020randomized_metamaterial]. To interrogate this encoder without perturbing it, we use a laser Doppler vibrometer (LDV) as a non-contact readout rather than a contact sensor. LDV provides high-bandwidth, high-sensitivity vibration measurement [@rothberg2017ldv; @castellini2006ldv; @wagner2021_laser_microphone_calibration], whereas piezoelectric patches and accelerometers introduce local mass and stiffness perturbations that modify frequency-response functions and boundary conditions [@ewins2000modal; @bi2013transducer_mass_loading; @nassif2005ldv_contact_sensors]. This non-contact choice preserves the native structural response needed to test whether the fingerprint is an intrinsic property of the object rather than an artifact of the sensor itself (Fig. 1; Methods: Experimental setup and data acquisition).

This physical-encoder view leads to two falsifiable predictions. First, the angle-to-spectrum mapping should be governed by a limited number of dominant physical channels rather than by an irreducibly high-dimensional response. Second, if the mechanism is genuine, the encoding should remain exploitable under realistic content variation and moderate noise. Natural structural responses are, however, dispersive and overlapping, so classical greedy decoding can fail under those conditions. Robust inference must therefore respect the geometry of the angle manifold. These predictions turn the problem from ad hoc single-sensor localization into a testable model of physical encoding and decoding.

Guided by these predictions, we establish that everyday objects act as reproducible direction-dependent encoders measurable from a single optical spot (Fig. 1); we show that the resulting angle-frequency response is low-rank and motivates a structured sparse inverse problem (Fig. 2); and we find that the encoding survives content variation even when dispersive overlap defeats classical decoding (Fig. 3). We then derive a physics-guided unrolled solver with learnable routing to resolve that ambiguity by exploiting the geometry of the physical angle manifold (Figs. 4 and 5). Finally, we show that the same physical-encoding view and inference recipe reproduce across five structurally distinct everyday objects under per-object calibration and retraining (Fig. 6). Together, these results position structural complexity as a computational resource for non-contact single-point acoustic sensing.

## Results

### Everyday objects act as direction-dependent spectral filters (Fig. 1)
Everyday objects transform incident sound into direction-dependent spectral fingerprints measurable from a single non-contact point (Fig. 1b). When driven by broadband white noise, the flat source spectrum is reshaped differently at each incidence angle (Fig. 1c): the structure acts as a direction-dependent spectral filter whose transfer function \(H(\theta, f)\) imprints a unique spectral signature for each direction [@rotter2017complex_media; @kuttruff2025room_acoustics]. A non-contact LDV provides the single-point readout while preserving the target's native boundary conditions (Methods: Experimental setup and data acquisition), ensuring that the measured frequency-response structure reflects the object's intrinsic dynamics rather than sensor-induced loading [@ewins2000modal; @bi2013transducer_mass_loading; @nassif2005ldv_contact_sensors].

These fingerprints are highly repeatable. Independent white-noise recordings at the same angle produce nearly identical spectra (Fig. 1d), with trial-to-trial variability far smaller than the between-angle differences. The spectral fingerprint is therefore a deterministic physical property of the structure rather than a noise artifact; quantitative discriminability analysis is provided in Fig. 3. The directional encoding is also frequency-selective: different frequency bands exhibit distinct angular response patterns (Fig. 1e), consistent with the excitation of multiple dispersive structural modes whose relative amplitudes depend on incidence direction (Fig. 2b,c).

### Spectral fingerprints arise from a low-dimensional physical manifold (Fig. 2)
The measured angle-frequency response exhibits a pronounced low-dimensional structure: the singular spectrum decays rapidly, indicating that only a small number of dominant spectral-spatial channels account for most direction-dependent variability (Fig. 2a-c). We organize these measurements into a response matrix \(H\in\mathbb{R}^{F\times E}\), where \(F\) denotes the number of frequency bins and \(E = 37\) the number of candidate angles. The effective rank is \(r = 3\) (Fig. 2a), capturing 91.9% of the singular-value energy. The full angle-frequency structure of \(H\) (Fig. 2d) and the rank-\(r\) reconstruction quality (Fig. 2e) confirm that low-rank compression retains the essential directional information.

This low-rank structure has a physical origin. Under small-amplitude dynamics, an everyday object acts as a linear system whose single-point velocity response can be expressed as a superposition of dispersive structural modes, each with its own spectral signature \(s_m(\omega)\) and direction-dependent coupling strength \(\alpha_m(\theta)\):

$$Y(\omega;\theta) \approx \sum_{m} s_m(\omega)\,\alpha_m(\theta), \qquad (1)$$

where \(Y(\omega;\theta)\) is the complex frequency response at the LDV measurement point for incidence direction \(\theta\), and the sum runs over a limited set of contributing modes (Supplementary Methods 4) [@ewins2000modal; @meirovitch2001fundamentals]. Because only a small number of modes contribute substantially, the resulting angle-frequency map is inherently low-dimensional. Even after compressing phase into magnitude statistics, the fingerprints retain this low effective dimensionality (Fig. 2a-c). The inter-angle correlation matrix (Fig. 2f) reveals a smooth manifold structure in which nearby angles share similar fingerprints, a geometric property exploited by the learned router (Fig. 5b).

The rapid concentration of singular-value energy motivates a compressed inference space. We construct an angle-indexed dictionary \(H = [h_1, \dots, h_E]\) by stacking empirical prototype fingerprints estimated from white-noise calibration, one column per candidate direction (Fig. 2d; Methods: Calibration protocol and outer-fold cross-validation). Given a new observation projected into the dominant singular-value decomposition (SVD) subspace, direction-of-arrival (DOA) inference reduces to a structured sparse inverse problem:

$$z \approx A\,x, \quad \text{subject to} \quad \lVert x \rVert_0 \le K, \qquad (2)$$

where \(z = U_r^\top \tilde{y}\) is the projected observation, \(A = U_r^\top H\) is the projected dictionary, \(x\) is a sparse coefficient vector over candidate directions, and \(K\) is the pursuit depth (Methods: Inference algorithms and network architecture). Projecting into the rank-\(r\) subspace focuses inference on the physically significant channels while suppressing noise (Fig. 2a-b; Methods: Physical dictionary formulation and subspace projection).

### Encoding survives content variation but classical decoding fails (Fig. 3)
Before introducing the learned solver, we ask whether the directional encoding established in Figs. 1-2 survives the transition from controlled white-noise calibration to realistic speech signals, and whether a classical solver can exploit it.

Under white-noise excitation, within-angle Pearson correlation is near-perfect (\(\bar{r} = 1.000\)) and well separated from between-angle correlation (\(\bar{r} = 0.724\); Cohen's \(d = 2.83\); Fig. 3a). Under speech, content variation reduces the within-angle correlation to \(\bar{r} = 0.907\) but the separation remains highly significant (\(d = 1.95\), \(p < 10^{-4}\); Fig. 3b). To quantify this more precisely, we compute a per-angle discriminability margin (within \(\bar{r}\) minus between \(\bar{r}\)) for both stimulus types: white noise yields a mean margin of 0.28, while speech retains a positive margin of 0.11 at every angle (Fig. 3c). The encoding is therefore degraded but not destroyed by content variation.

Yet classical orthogonal matching pursuit (OMP) decoding fails severely: it performs well on white-noise fingerprints but collapses to near-chance accuracy on held-out speech clips (Fig. 3d). The split-triangle pairwise similarity matrix (Fig. 3e) visualizes the underlying cause. White noise produces a near-identity matrix amenable to greedy pursuit, whereas speech produces a diffuse but structured manifold that requires a geometry-aware solver. A dose-response analysis across signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) levels (Fig. 3f) confirms that content variation is the causal factor: OMP accuracy declines monotonically as additive noise increases in both the white-noise and speech conditions. Together, these results localize the bottleneck: encoding is robust, but classical pursuit cannot exploit the residual discriminability under content variation. A physics-guided solver capable of navigating the angle manifold is therefore needed (Fig. 4).

### Physics-guided routing resolves dispersive ambiguity (Fig. 4)
Dispersive fingerprints are informative but intrinsically ambiguous: multiple candidate angles can produce partially overlapping spectral responses. Orthogonal matching pursuit (OMP), a classical greedy solver, selects the dictionary entry most correlated with the current measurement residual [@tropp2007omp; @tropp2004greed]. As shown in Fig. 3d, single-shot OMP remains near chance on held-out speech despite the preserved encoding discriminability established in Fig. 3a-c, and the dose-response analysis (Fig. 3f) confirms that content variation is the causal factor. Content-induced spectral variation causes classical greedy methods to spread selection mass across overlapping responses, motivating a solver that can learn from the structure of the angle manifold.

We address this by deriving a physics-guided neural solver that unrolls the iterative pursuit algorithm into a trainable network (Fig. 4a; Methods: Inference algorithms and network architecture) [@gregor2010lista; @monga2021unrolling]. At each stage, the solver computes a physically grounded correlation between each dictionary entry and the remaining unexplained signal. Instead of committing to the single best match as OMP does, the learned router assigns soft weight across neighboring angle hypotheses using the geometry already present in the dictionary. The resulting weighting gates a sparse update, after which the explained portion is subtracted to maintain consistency with the physical dictionary (Supplementary Methods 3). The number of iterative selection steps \(K\) (the pursuit depth) provides a controlled residual-correction budget under noise and feature mismatch, and is shared between OMP and the learned solver for fair comparison.

Training converges stably under the fixed 20-epoch schedule (Fig. 4b). A systematic ablation at clean SNR (Fig. 4c) compares the full model against ablation variants, confirming that transformer routing, type bias, and physics-grounded gating each contribute measurably, with performance degrading progressively as components are removed. The solver achieves broadly uniform held-out accuracy across the full angular grid (Fig. 4d), indicating no systematic blind spots.

When the learned weights concentrate on a single entry, the update reduces to classical greedy selection; when they spread across nearby angles, the solver exploits the smooth structure of the angle manifold to regularize its choices. The mechanistic basis of this routing behavior and its consequences under noise are examined in Fig. 5. The sparse-reconstruction viewpoint adopted here parallels classical sparse DOA methods that discretize a propagation manifold and solve for a sparse angular spectrum [@malioutov2005sparse_doa; @baraniuk2007compressive_sensing].

### Learned routing aligns with physical structure and resists noise (Fig. 5)
The central finding of this work is that the learned attention router recovers structure already present in the physical dictionary, rather than learning an arbitrary pattern.

Manifold-aligned routing confers noise robustness at the task level (Fig. 5a). On held-out speech recordings, the physics-aware model outperforms both the no-transformer ablation and the analytical OMP baseline across signal-to-noise ratio levels. To understand the mechanistic basis of this robustness, we next examine the attention router itself.

The physical angle manifold exhibits characteristic correlations in the dictionary \(H\): nearby angles share spectral features, producing a banded correlation structure. Notably, the learned query-key (QK) correlation of the attention router mirrors this physical structure with even sharper diagonal locality (Fig. 5b). The correlation between the upper-triangular entries of the dictionary correlation and QK correlation matrices is statistically significant, confirming that the router respects the geometry of the physical angle manifold rather than fitting an unconstrained attention pattern. The alignment between learned routing and physical structure is the core interpretability result.

The consequences of this alignment are visible in the selection statistics (Fig. 5c). Across the full angle grid, OMP spreads selection mass over a persistent off-diagonal set of dictionary entries, reflecting the spectral overlap that confuses greedy pursuit. By contrast, the physics-aware model concentrates selection probability sharply along the diagonal, indicating that it selects the correct direction for each incidence angle with high consistency. The diagonal concentration mirrors the sharpened QK structure, demonstrating that manifold-aligned routing translates directly into cleaner direction selection.

Confusion matrices (Fig. 5d), angle-specific routing distributions (Fig. 5e), and per-angle improvement quantification (Fig. 5f) show that this structure-aligned behavior persists from the global angle manifold down to individual directions. Full ablation variants and band-resolved diagnostics are provided in Supplementary Methods 1-5.

### Physical encoding is universal across everyday materials (Fig. 6)
The preceding analyses (Figs. 1-5) used a single acrylic plate as the sensing target. A natural question is whether the low-rank encoding mechanism and manifold-aligned routing are specific to one engineered surface or reflect a general consequence of dispersive wave superposition. We test this by repeating the full pipeline on five structurally diverse everyday objects spanning a broad range of damping, stiffness, and geometric complexity: an acrylic plate, a paper cup, a wooden cutting board, a cardboard box, and a laptop shell (Fig. 6a). Each object undergoes independent white-noise calibration, dictionary construction, model retraining, and speech evaluation.

All five targets produce direction-dependent dispersion signatures visible in their dictionary heatmaps (Fig. 6b), confirming that the spectral-filtering phenomenon established in Fig. 1 is not material-specific. Under the per-object calibrate-and-retrain protocol (Methods: Calibration protocol and outer-fold cross-validation), the physics-aware model maintains lower root-mean-square error (RMSE) than analytical OMP across the object set (Fig. 6c). The performance gap widens on targets with higher damping and geometric complexity, where content-induced spectral overlap becomes more severe and further exposes the limitations of greedy pursuit identified in Fig. 3d-f.

The universality has a physical basis independent of solver accuracy. Although specific material parameters (bending stiffness \(D_p\), density \(\rho\)) shift resonance frequencies and mode shapes, all targets share the same underlying mechanism: linear superposition of a limited set of dispersive structural modes. The per-band SVD spectra (Fig. 6d) confirm this directly: singular-value decay is consistently rapid across five frequency bands, indicating that the low-rank concentration is a frequency-distributed physical property rather than a narrowband resonance artifact. Band-resolved routing accuracy (Fig. 6e) further shows that the physics-aware model outperforms OMP in every frequency band, demonstrating that the learned solver exploits the shared sparse-superposition structure across the spectrum. Together, these results support the interpretation that the solver learns the physical logic encoded by the dictionary \(H\): dispersive mode superposition and manifold smoothness, rather than memorizing target-specific trial statistics.

## Discussion
These results establish a complementary sensing paradigm in which passive everyday structures function as naturally randomized physical encoders for sound. A single, non-contact vibration readout contains enough information to decode DOA because the object converts incidence direction into a reproducible spectral fingerprint (Fig. 1), shifting the sensing footprint from an array aperture to a single optical spot. The paradigm aligns with the broader recognition that disorder and scattering in complex media can serve as a computational resource rather than an obstacle to sensing [@gigan2022imaging_computing_disorder; @rotter2017complex_media]. Prior implementations of this principle have relied on engineered disordered structures, such as randomized resonant metamaterials for compressive vibration identification in the elastic domain [@jiang2020randomized_metamaterial] and programmable coding metasurfaces for single-pixel compressive DOA estimation in the electromagnetic domain [@hoang2021single_pixel_doa]. We show that unmodified everyday objects already possess sufficient structural complexity to encode spatial information in the acoustic domain, without requiring purpose-built metamaterial designs.

The physical basis of the encoding is the direction-dependent superposition of dispersive structural modes. In this modal view, incidence direction changes the relative participation of a limited set of modes, yielding a low-rank angle-frequency response whose singular spectrum decays rapidly (Fig. 2a,b), consistent with eigenchannel interpretations of complex media [@popoff2010transmission_matrix; @mosk2012complex_media] and with analogous low-rank concentration observed in engineered randomized metamaterials [@jiang2020randomized_metamaterial]. Our results extend this observation to natural, unmodified structures and, across five structurally distinct targets (Fig. 6), suggest that low effective dimensionality is a generic consequence of finite modal density rather than a property of a single engineered panel. This interpretation depends on preserving the native structural response: because the optical readout does not mechanically load the target, the fingerprints can be attributed to the object itself rather than to a sensor-induced artifact [@ewins2000modal; @bi2013transducer_mass_loading]. Projecting into the dominant subspace improves robustness by retaining the physically informative variation while suppressing noise; however, as damping increases, modal overlap grows and directional identifiability can degrade [@ewins2000modal; @inman2013engineering_vibration].

That modal overlap, compounded by content-induced variation in speech, is what causes classical greedy pursuit to fail and spread spurious off-axis correlations (Fig. 3d-f). The physics-guided unrolled solver addresses this ambiguity by learning a soft routing distribution that regularizes selection using context across the entire angle manifold while preserving residual-consistent updates anchored to the physical dictionary (Fig. 4a). The approach embeds domain knowledge as structural inductive bias rather than learning from data alone, consistent with the broader framework of physics-informed machine learning [@karniadakis2021piml; @gregor2010lista; @monga2021unrolling]. Relative to non-negative matrix factorization (NMF)-based single-sensor localization and black-box deep neural network (DNN) classifiers, the key advantage is mechanistic interpretability: the learned routing aligns with the physical angle manifold (Fig. 5b), concentrates selection sharply on the correct direction (Fig. 5c), and persists across frequency bands (Fig. 6e; Supplementary Methods 1-5). The solver therefore succeeds by exploiting manifold smoothness already present in the dictionary, stabilizing inference under noise and nonstationary speech (Fig. 5a).

Beyond our specific implementation, the same array-free sensing idea has been explored in several modalities. El Badawy and Dokmanić showed that a single microphone embedded in an ad hoc LEGO scatterer can localize sound sources via NMF of the direction-dependent transfer function [@elbadawy2018lego_doa], whereas DiPassio et al. and subsequent work used contact-mounted piezoelectric sensing to decode structure-borne directional signatures from panels and beams [@dipassio2023doa_single_sensor; @rutowski2024reverb_single_sensor; @rutowski2025_structural_beamforming]. These modalities are complementary: contact sensors and embedded microphones offer compact form factors suited to integrated devices, whereas non-contact readout is preferable when preserving the encoder's physical integrity is essential or when the target cannot be instrumented.

Several constraints bound the present demonstration. The angular grid spans a half-plane (0-180°) with 5° resolution; extending to full azimuthal or elevation coverage will require additional calibration geometry and may encounter symmetry-related ambiguities. All experiments were conducted in a controlled acoustic environment with a single static source; real-world deployment would need to contend with reverberation, multiple simultaneous sources, and moving emitters. The LDV, while ensuring measurement fidelity, is a laboratory instrument that limits field deployment; future work could explore whether emerging compact laser vibrometers or high-bandwidth microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) alternatives retain sufficient sensitivity. Per-object calibration and retraining are currently required (Fig. 6); whether transferable representations can reduce this overhead remains an open question. Even under corpus-internal held-out folds, generalization to a separately collected external speech corpus remains untested.

The central open frontier is whether invariances of the angle-frequency manifold can be identified across objects, enabling calibration-efficient or few-shot transfer. A second frontier is to quantify information-theoretic limits of single-point decoding as fingerprint separability degrades with increasing damping or angular density (Fig. 2a), and to determine how mounting conditions and LDV spot placement trade off robustness versus sensitivity. Extending the framework to multi-source and moving-source regimes will require modeling how fingerprints superpose and evolve across time windows. More broadly, the principle that passive structural complexity can serve as a computational resource for wave-field inference suggests applications beyond acoustics, including vibration-based structural health monitoring, seismic source localization, and compact sensing for internet-of-things (IoT) and assistive-technology devices where conventional arrays are impractical.

## Methods

### Experimental setup and data acquisition
All processing and learning were implemented in Python with PyTorch and trained on Apple Silicon (MPS). Unless otherwise stated, NumPy and PyTorch random seeds were fixed to 42.

Experiments used a single loudspeaker scanned over a half-plane angular grid comprising 37 incidence directions from 0° to 180° in 5° increments. Out-of-plane surface velocity was measured at a fixed LDV spot on each object while the mounting configuration was held fixed throughout calibration and evaluation for that object. All downstream processing used waveforms resampled to 16,000 Hz, and each clip was approximately 3 s long.

Two excitation regimes were used: (i) broadband white-noise playback for dictionary calibration and fingerprint repeatability diagnostics, and (ii) speech recordings for end-to-end DOA decoding and robustness experiments. The four frequency bands in Fig. 1e (0.3-0.5, 0.5-1, 1-2, and 2-3 kHz) tile the analysis band and were analyzed separately to probe frequency-dependent dispersive behavior. Five objects were selected to span a range of damping and structural complexity: acrylic plate, paper cup, wooden board, cardboard box, and laptop shell.

### Signal processing and feature extraction
The LDV measures out-of-plane surface velocity \(V(x_L,y_L,\omega)=i\omega W(x_L,y_L,\omega)\), where \(W\) is the displacement field and \((x_L, y_L)\) denotes the fixed laser measurement location. For incidence direction \(\theta\), the complex single-point response is \(Y(\omega;\theta)=V(x_L,y_L,\omega)\). For each recorded clip, a short-time Fourier transform (Hann window, 2,048 samples, hop 512) is computed and collapsed into a time-averaged power spectrum:

$$\widehat{S}(\omega_k;\theta)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_t |V[k,t]|^2, \qquad (3)$$

where \(V[k,t]\) is the complex STFT coefficient at frequency bin \(k\) and frame \(t\), and \(T\) is the number of retained frames. The spectrum is restricted to [300, 3,000] Hz (\(F = 346\) bins). Because the observed fingerprints are magnitude statistics, each clip is summarized by a log-power feature vector \(y[k]=\log_{10}(\widehat{S}+\epsilon)\), then standardized per frequency bin using white-noise calibration statistics to yield a normalized feature \(\tilde{y}[k]\) (Supplementary Methods 1). These normalized log-power fingerprints serve as the stable angle-indexed features used for downstream inference, so the sparse model in Results (Eq. 2) is interpreted as an approximation in normalized feature space rather than as a literal complex-domain superposition law.

### Physical dictionary formulation and subspace projection
The normalized features serve as the basis for constructing the angle-indexed dictionary used in inference. Per-angle prototypes are averaged across calibration trials to form columns \(h_e\), where \(e\) indexes one of the \(E\) candidate directions, yielding the dictionary \(H=[h_1,\dots,h_{37}]\in\mathbb{R}^{F\times E}\), with \(E=37\). Singular-value decomposition (SVD) \(H=U\Sigma V^\top\) reveals that the rank-\(r=3\) subspace captures 91.9% of the singular-value energy (Fig. 2a).

For projected inference, both observations and dictionary are mapped into this low-rank subspace: \(z=U_r^\top \tilde{y}\) and \(A=U_r^\top H\). This projection provides the compressed representation used in the sparse inverse problem introduced in Results (Eq. 2) and focuses inference on the physically significant channels while suppressing noise.

### Inference algorithms and network architecture
Using the sparse inverse problem introduced in Results (Eq. 2), DOA inference seeks a sparse coefficient vector over the projected dictionary \(A\). The formulation rests on three assumptions: (1) over each analysis window the target behaves as a linear time-invariant system, so direction-dependent responses superpose in the frequency domain; (2) \(Hx\) is a sparse prototype approximation in standardized feature space, not a literal power-additivity law; and (3) SVD projection preserves angle-indexed structure while suppressing noise. The representative Kirchhoff-Love plate operator and Green's function derivation supporting assumption (1) are given in Supplementary Methods 4.

In the analytical orthogonal matching pursuit (OMP) baseline, the dictionary atom most correlated with the current residual is iteratively selected, added to the support set, and its contribution removed by least-squares refit and residual subtraction. After \(K=2\) stages, the predicted direction is \(\hat\theta=\theta_{\arg\max_e |x[e]|}\); the full recursive definition is given in Supplementary Methods 2. As shown in Results (Fig. 3d), OMP performs strongly on white noise but fails under speech content variation, motivating a learnable alternative.

The physics-guided solver unrolls the same \(K\)-stage pursuit into a differentiable network with learned attention-based routing. The residual is initialized as \(r_0 = z\) and the sparse coefficient vector as \(x_0 = 0\). At each stage \(t = 1,\dots,K\), the physical match score between the residual and every dictionary atom is computed as:

$$g_t = A^\top r_t, \qquad (4)$$

where \(r_t\) is the current residual and each entry of \(g_t\) quantifies how well a candidate direction explains the unexplained observation through the physical dictionary. Rather than selecting the single best-matching atom as in OMP, a transformer encoder (embedding dimension \(d_\mathrm{model}=128\), 2 attention heads, 1 encoder layer) computes query and key projections of \(g_t\) and produces soft routing weights via scaled dot-product attention:

$$w_t = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{Q_t\,K_t^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) g_t, \qquad (5)$$

where \(Q_t = g_t W^Q\) and \(K_t = g_t W^K\) are learned linear projections and \(d_k\) is the key dimension. In this setting, \(w_t\) acts as a manifold-aware routing distribution over neighboring angle hypotheses rather than a sequence-model token weight. The routing weights modulate the match scores to produce a gated sparse update:

$$\Delta x_t = w_t \odot g_t, \qquad (6)$$

where \(\odot\) denotes element-wise multiplication. The sparse coefficient vector is accumulated as \(x_{t+1} = x_t + \eta\,\Delta x_t\), where \(\eta\) is a learned step-size parameter that scales each stage-wise sparse update before residual correction, and the residual is corrected by dictionary-consistent subtraction:

$$r_{t+1} = r_t - A\,\Delta x_t, \qquad (7)$$

ensuring that each stage explains a portion of the observation through the physical dictionary. After the final stage, expert-level logits are obtained by L2 aggregation of atom-level coefficients within each angular direction, yielding a probability vector:

$$p[e] = \frac{\lVert x_K^{(e)} \rVert_2}{\sum_{e'} \lVert x_K^{(e')} \rVert_2}, \qquad (8)$$

where \(x_K^{(e)}\) denotes the sub-vector of \(x_K\) corresponding to direction \(e \in \{1,\dots,E\}\). The predicted DOA is \(\hat\theta = \theta_{\arg\max_e\, p[e]}\). The routing weights \(w_t\) can be directly inspected as mechanistic evidence of manifold-aligned selection (Fig. 5b,c); Supplementary Methods 3 provides the complete per-stage equations.

The network is trained to minimize a composite loss combining three terms: a cross-entropy classification loss \(\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{cls}\) over the direction probabilities \(p[e]\), a reconstruction loss \(\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{rec} = \lVert r_K \rVert_2^2\) that penalizes unexplained residual energy and thereby enforces consistency with the physical dictionary, and a monotonicity regularizer \(\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{mono}\) that encourages sparse coefficients to concentrate on the selected direction across successive stages:

$$\mathcal{L} = \alpha\,\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{cls} + \beta\,\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{rec} + \gamma\,\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{mono}, \qquad (9)$$

with \(\alpha=1.0\), \(\beta=0.2\), and \(\gamma=0.5\). Optimization is performed with Adam (learning rate \(10^{-3}\), weight decay \(10^{-4}\)) for 20 epochs with batch size 32.

### Calibration protocol and outer-fold cross-validation
The dictionary \(H\) described above is constructed exclusively from white-noise calibration recordings, using all 3 clips per angle. The transfer function \(H(\theta,f)\) was estimated as the geometric-mean magnitude ratio of the LDV response to the source signal in the STFT domain, averaged over calibration trials. Calibration clips are never used for training or evaluation of the DOA decoder.

For speech decoding (Figs. 4-5), each angle contributes 260 speech clips (9,620 total). Clips are assigned deterministically to five outer folds by the rule \(\mathrm{fold} = \mathrm{clip\_id} \bmod 5\), producing 52 clips per angle per fold. For outer fold \(f\), fold \(f\) serves as the held-out test set, fold \((f+1) \bmod 5\) serves as the validation set, and the remaining three folds form the training set, yielding 5,772/1,924/1,924 train/validation/test samples in total. Model architecture, loss weights, optimizer, and the 20-epoch training schedule are fixed across folds; best-epoch selection uses only the validation fold. All speech results use the fixed \(H\) estimated from white-noise calibration only, and scalar performance metrics are reported from held-out outer-test predictions.

For cross-material experiments (Fig. 6), the full pipeline is repeated per target: object-specific white-noise calibration data are acquired, the corresponding \(H\) is estimated, and a separate model is retrained under the same outer-fold protocol. Universality is assessed at the level of a shared physical mechanism and inference recipe, not by reusing weights across objects.

### Discriminability analysis
Before evaluating the learned solver, encoding quality is quantified independently of any inference algorithm (Fig. 3). White-noise and speech stimuli are compared using the same dictionary \(H\). For each stimulus type, within-angle Pearson correlation (mean pairwise \(\rho\) among clips at the same angle) and between-angle correlation (mean pairwise \(\rho\) across different angles) are computed. The discriminability margin is defined as within \(\bar{r}\) minus between \(\bar{r}\); a positive margin indicates that the encoding preserves directional information (Fig. 3c). Effect sizes are reported as Cohen's \(d\) and significance via the two-sided Mann-Whitney \(U\) test. Formal definitions are given in Supplementary Methods 5.

### Dose-response and noise robustness
For the dose-response analysis (Fig. 3f), noise levels are swept in two conditions: (i) white-noise signal with additive speech-spectrum noise, and (ii) speech signal with additive babble noise. For noise-robustness evaluation, zero-mean white noise is added at SNR levels \(\infty\), 30, 20, 15, 10, 5, and 0 dB in the time domain, using the same feature extraction and dictionary for each level.

### Solver ablations
For learned-solver ablations, three variants are tested with all other components held fixed: *no-transformer* replaces the transformer router with an identity bypass, *fixed heuristic* replaces learned routing with OMP-based selection, and *dense routing* uses uniform weighting over all experts.

### Evaluation metrics and statistics
Top-1 accuracy is reported on the discrete angle grid. Angular error is the minimal angular difference \(\Delta(\hat\theta,\theta)=\min_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|\hat\theta-\theta+360k|\), and root-mean-square error (RMSE) = \(\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_i \Delta(\hat\theta_i,\theta_i)^2}\). Unless noted otherwise, scalar speech-decoder metrics are reported as mean ± s.d. across the five outer test folds. Noise-sweep curves average over five independent noise realizations per SNR level; error bars denote standard error of the mean (SEM) across noise realizations. Mechanistic visualizations such as training curves, confusion matrices, and routing maps are shown for representative folds, whereas aggregate performance statements in the main text refer to held-out outer-fold summaries. When statistical hypothesis tests are used, the test, sidedness, and multiple-comparison correction are stated alongside the corresponding figure panel. Effect sizes are reported as Cohen's \(d\); distribution comparisons use the two-sided Mann-Whitney \(U\) test.
