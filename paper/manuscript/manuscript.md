# Non-contact acoustic sensing via the natural physical encoding of everyday objects

## Abstract
Conventional acoustic direction sensing relies on microphone arrays, limiting miniaturization and deployment in harsh or constrained environments. Here we show that everyday objects can themselves act as direction-dependent physical encoders: incident sound excites dispersive structural vibrations whose superposition produces a single-point spectral fingerprint measurable by a non-contact laser Doppler vibrometer. Modeling the angle-frequency response as a response matrix reveals a rapidly decaying singular-value spectrum, indicating a limited number of dominant encoding channels. This low-rank structure motivates a sparse inverse formulation over a calibrated dictionary of angle-specific spectral templates. To solve this inverse problem, we use a physics-guided neural solver with learned routing while preserving consistency with the physical dictionary. Under held-out speech evaluation on a 37-angle grid spanning 0-180° with 5° resolution, the solver sustains robust decoding down to 0 dB signal-to-noise ratio, and its learned routing structure aligns with the physical angle manifold, providing an interpretable link between data-driven inference and the underlying modal physics. Across five everyday targets, we reproduce direction-dependent fingerprints and find that downstream sensing quality varies substantially across objects, requiring material screening even when structured physical encoding is present.

## Introduction
Extracting spatial information from wave fields is a recurring challenge spanning acoustics, optics, and seismology. In real environments, structural dispersion, multiple scattering, and boundary reverberation entangle propagation paths and produce seemingly chaotic time-domain signals [@rotter2017complex_media; @kuttruff2025room_acoustics]. Classical direction-of-arrival (DOA) estimation therefore relies on spatial sampling with sensor arrays and analytical inversion, including beamforming [@capon1969fkw], subspace methods such as MUSIC and ESPRIT [@schmidt1986music; @roy1989esprit], and microphone-array processing techniques reviewed in [@krim1996array; @vantrees2002optimum; @johnson1993array; @brandstein2001microphone_arrays; @benesty2008microphone_array]. However, the need for an aperture imposes severe constraints on size and placement, limiting integration in wearable platforms, structural monitoring systems, and compact devices deployed in harsh or space-constrained environments [@grumiaux2022_ssl_survey_deep_learning]. These constraints motivate a direction-sensing mechanism that does not depend on a spatial aperture.

Single-sensor alternatives suggest that such a mechanism may exist, but their physical basis remains unresolved. El Badawy and Dokmanić demonstrated that a single microphone embedded in an ad hoc LEGO scatterer can localize sound sources by exploiting a direction-dependent transfer function [@elbadawy2018lego_doa]. More recent studies show that a single structural vibration measurement can carry directional cues because acoustic incidence excites structure-borne waves whose response depends on incidence direction [@dipassio2022_audio_capture_structural_sensors; @dipassio2023_waspaa_wake_word; @dipassio2023doa_single_sensor; @rutowski2024reverb_single_sensor; @rutowski2025_structural_beamforming]. In parallel, sparse reconstruction offers an alternative DOA viewpoint by discretizing an angular manifold and recovering a sparse angular spectrum from compressed measurements [@malioutov2005sparse_doa; @donoho2006compressed_sensing; @candes2006robust_uncertainty; @candes2008compressive_sampling]. Convex relaxations such as basis pursuit and \(\ell_1\)-regularized regression provide classical baselines [@chen2001basis_pursuit; @tibshirani1996lasso], while greedy pursuit algorithms including matching pursuit and orthogonal matching pursuit (OMP) offer efficient solvers that retain interpretability [@mallat1993matching_pursuits; @pati1993omp; @tropp2007omp]. Array-free alternatives also exist in the form of acoustic vector sensors, which infer DOA from collocated pressure-particle-velocity measurements but require specialized instrumentation [@nehorai1994vector_sensor]. What remains unclear is whether these directional cues reflect a reproducible, physically compressible encoding mechanism that can be calibrated systematically across distinct everyday targets rather than target-specific idiosyncrasies.

To test this possibility, we recast everyday objects as physical encoders that convert acoustic incidence direction into a direction-dependent superposition of dispersive structural vibrations and, in turn, into a single-point spectral fingerprint. This perspective is inspired by computational wave physics, where complexity can be harnessed rather than suppressed, as in time-reversed acoustics [@fink1997time; @draeger1997one], single-pixel imaging [@duarte2008single], structural health monitoring [@ing2008lamb], transmission-matrix and wavefront-shaping views of complex media [@popoff2010transmission_matrix; @mosk2012complex_media; @rotter2017complex_media], and randomized metamaterials designed for compressive vibration identification [@jiang2020randomized_metamaterial]. To interrogate this encoder without perturbing it, we use a laser Doppler vibrometer (LDV) as a non-contact readout rather than a contact sensor. LDV provides high-bandwidth, high-sensitivity vibration measurement [@rothberg2017ldv; @castellini2006ldv; @wagner2021_laser_microphone_calibration], whereas piezoelectric patches and accelerometers introduce local mass and stiffness perturbations that modify frequency-response functions and boundary conditions [@ewins2000modal; @bi2013transducer_mass_loading; @nassif2005ldv_contact_sensors]. This non-contact choice preserves the native structural response needed to test whether the fingerprint is an intrinsic property of the object rather than an artifact of the sensor itself (Fig. 1; Methods: Experimental setup and data acquisition).

This physical-encoder view leads to two falsifiable predictions. First, the angle-to-spectrum mapping should be governed by a limited number of dominant physical channels rather than by an irreducibly high-dimensional response. Second, if the mechanism is genuine, the encoding should remain exploitable under realistic content variation and moderate noise. Natural structural responses are, however, dispersive and overlapping, so classical greedy decoding can fail under those conditions. Robust inference must therefore respect the geometry of the angle manifold. These predictions turn the problem from ad hoc single-sensor localization into a testable model of physical encoding and decoding.

Guided by these predictions, we establish that everyday objects act as reproducible direction-dependent encoders measurable from a single optical spot (Fig. 1); we show that the resulting angle-frequency response is low-rank and motivates a structured sparse inverse problem (Fig. 2); and we find that the encoding survives content variation even when dispersive overlap defeats classical decoding (Fig. 3). We then derive a physics-guided unrolled solver with learnable routing to resolve that ambiguity by exploiting the geometry of the physical angle manifold (Figs. 4 and 5). Finally, we show that structured direction-dependent encoding and low-rank continuity reproduce across five structurally distinct everyday objects under object-specific calibration, and that cross-material screening differences are explained better by frequency-localized directional structure than by overall response energy alone (Fig. 6). Together, these results position structural complexity as a computational resource for non-contact single-point acoustic sensing.

## Results

### Everyday objects act as direction-dependent spectral filters (Fig. 1)
As schematized in Fig. 1b and demonstrated experimentally in Fig. 1c-e, everyday objects transform incident sound into direction-dependent spectral fingerprints measurable from a single non-contact point. When driven by broadband white noise, the flat source spectrum is reshaped differently at each incidence angle (Fig. 1c): the structure acts as a direction-dependent spectral filter whose transfer function \(H(\theta, f)\) imprints a unique spectral signature for each direction [@rotter2017complex_media; @kuttruff2025room_acoustics]. A non-contact LDV provides the single-point readout while preserving the target's native boundary conditions (Methods: Experimental setup and data acquisition), ensuring that the measured frequency-response structure reflects the object's intrinsic dynamics rather than sensor-induced loading [@ewins2000modal; @bi2013transducer_mass_loading; @nassif2005ldv_contact_sensors].

These fingerprints are highly repeatable. Independent white-noise recordings at the same angle produce nearly identical spectra (Fig. 1d), with trial-to-trial variability far smaller than the between-angle differences. The spectral fingerprint is therefore a deterministic physical property of the structure rather than a noise artifact; quantitative discriminability analysis is provided in Fig. 3. The directional encoding is also frequency-selective: different frequency bands exhibit distinct angular response patterns (Fig. 1e), consistent with the excitation of multiple dispersive structural modes whose relative amplitudes depend on incidence direction (Fig. 2b,c).

### Spectral fingerprints arise from a low-dimensional physical manifold (Fig. 2)
The measured angle-frequency response exhibits a concentrated low-dimensional structure: the singular spectrum decays rapidly across the centered-magnitude dictionary (Fig. 2a-c). The first six modes capture 80.3% of the centered-magnitude energy across the calibrated angle manifold. Extending to eight modes raises the cumulative fraction to 85.1%. The cumulative DOA-capacity curve in Fig. 2a tracks the same early saturation, indicating that most angular variation is concentrated in the first few modes. The continuous transfer function \(H(\theta, f)\) introduced in Fig. 1 can be sampled on the \(F\) analysis frequencies and \(E = 37\) calibration angles to form an angle-indexed response matrix \(H\in\mathbb{R}^{F\times E}\). The full angle-frequency structure of \(H\) (Fig. 2d) and the all-angle reconstruction fidelity curves for ranks 3, 5, and 6 (Fig. 2e) show that strong reconstruction fidelity is already reached within the same six-mode regime. Separately, the inter-angle fingerprint similarity matrix (Fig. 2f) shows that neighboring directions remain close in fingerprint space, revealing a smooth geometric organization across angles.

This low-rank structure has a physical origin. Under small-amplitude dynamics, an everyday object acts as a linear system whose single-point velocity response can be expressed as a superposition of dispersive structural modes, each with its own spectral signature \(s_m(\omega)\) and direction-dependent coupling strength \(\alpha_m(\theta)\):

$$Y(\omega;\theta) \approx \sum_{m} s_m(\omega)\,\alpha_m(\theta), \qquad (1)$$

where \(Y(\omega;\theta)\) is the complex frequency response at the LDV measurement point for incidence direction \(\theta\), and the sum runs over a limited set of contributing modes (Supplementary Methods 4) [@ewins2000modal; @meirovitch2001fundamentals]. Equation 1 is the continuous-domain physical model: the spectral templates \(s_m(\omega)\) define the dominant channels, while \(\alpha_m(\theta)\) changes their participation with incidence angle. In the experiment, however, we do not decode DOA directly from the complex response \(Y(\omega;\theta)\). Each clip is converted into a magnitude-based spectral fingerprint, discretized over frequency and angle, and averaged across calibration trials to form prototype columns \(h_e\). The matrix \(H=[h_1,\dots,h_E]\) is therefore the calibration-derived discrete counterpart of \(H(\theta, f)\), and it preserves the same low-dimensional modal structure even after phase is compressed into magnitude statistics (Fig. 2a-e). This same full dictionary also exhibits smooth angle-to-angle geometry: the inter-angle fingerprint similarity matrix (Fig. 2f) contains a near-diagonal high-similarity band, indicating that nearby angles share similar fingerprints, a physical property later exploited by the learned router (Fig. 5c).

This physical compression motivates a compressed inference space. A new observation is first summarized as a normalized log-power fingerprint \(\tilde{y}\), then projected into the dominant singular-value decomposition (SVD) subspace as \(z=U_r^\top \tilde{y}\). Applying the same projection to the calibration dictionary gives \(A=U_r^\top H\). In this projected feature space, direction-of-arrival (DOA) inference reduces to a structured sparse inverse problem:

$$z \approx A\,x, \quad \text{subject to} \quad \lVert x \rVert_0 \le K, \qquad (2)$$

where \(z\) is the projected test fingerprint, \(A\) is the projected physical dictionary, \(x\) is a sparse coefficient vector whose dominant support identifies the source direction, and \(K\) is the pursuit depth, that is, a small residual-correction budget for overlapping responses and noise rather than an assumption of multiple simultaneous sources (Methods: Inference algorithms and network architecture). Projecting into the rank-\(r\) subspace focuses inference on the physically significant channels while suppressing noise (Fig. 2a-b; Methods: Physical dictionary formulation and subspace projection).

### Encoding survives content variation but classical decoding fails (Fig. 3)
Before introducing the learned solver, we ask whether the directional encoding established in Figs. 1-2 survives the transition from controlled white-noise calibration to realistic speech signals, and whether a classical solver can exploit it.

Under white-noise excitation, within-angle Pearson correlation is near-perfect (\(\bar{r} = 1.000\)) and well separated from between-angle correlation (\(\bar{r} = 0.724\); Cohen's \(d = 2.83\); Fig. 3a). Under speech, content variation reduces the within-angle correlation to \(\bar{r} = 0.907\) but the separation remains highly significant (\(d = 1.95\), \(p < 10^{-4}\); Fig. 3b). To quantify this more precisely, we compute a per-angle discriminability margin (within \(\bar{r}\) minus between \(\bar{r}\)) for both stimulus types: white noise yields a mean margin of 0.28, while speech retains a positive margin of 0.11 at every angle (Fig. 3c, with light bootstrap uncertainty shading). The encoding is therefore degraded but not destroyed by content variation.

Yet classical orthogonal matching pursuit (OMP) decoding fails severely: angle-resolved OMP traces show that performance remains high for most white-noise fingerprints but collapses to near-chance levels across the calibrated grid for held-out speech clips (Fig. 3d, with clip-level uncertainty shading). The split-triangle pairwise fingerprint similarity map (Fig. 3e) visualizes the underlying cause. White noise produces a near-identity matrix amenable to greedy pursuit, whereas speech produces a diffuse but structured manifold that requires a geometry-aware solver. A dose-response analysis across signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) levels (Fig. 3f) confirms that content variation is the causal factor: OMP accuracy declines monotonically as additive noise increases in both the white-noise and speech conditions, with the white-noise curve shown with clip-level SEM shading and the speech-babble curve shown as a five-seed mean \(\pm\) SEM band. Together, these results localize the bottleneck: encoding is robust, but classical pursuit cannot exploit the residual discriminability under content variation. A physics-guided solver capable of navigating the angle manifold is therefore needed (Fig. 4).

### Physics-guided routing resolves dispersive ambiguity (Fig. 4)
Dispersive fingerprints are informative but intrinsically ambiguous: multiple candidate angles can produce partially overlapping spectral responses. In the projected sparse model of Eq. 2, this ambiguity appears as several nearby dictionary atoms with comparable correlations to the same observation. Orthogonal matching pursuit (OMP), a classical greedy solver, selects the dictionary entry most correlated with the current measurement residual [@tropp2007omp; @tropp2004greed]. As shown in Fig. 3d, single-shot OMP remains near chance on held-out speech despite the preserved encoding discriminability established in Fig. 3a-c, and the dose-response analysis (Fig. 3f) confirms that content variation is the causal factor. Content-induced spectral variation broadens these correlations, motivating a solver that operates on the same Eq. 2 inverse problem but can use the geometry of the angle manifold.

We address this by deriving a physics-guided neural solver that unrolls the iterative pursuit used to solve Eq. 2 into a trainable network (Fig. 4a; Methods: Inference algorithms and network architecture) [@gregor2010lista; @monga2021unrolling]. At each stage, the solver computes a physically grounded correlation between each dictionary entry and the remaining unexplained signal. Instead of committing to the single best match as OMP does, the learned router assigns soft weight across neighboring angle hypotheses using the geometry already present in the dictionary. These routing weights gate the sparse update in Eq. 2, after which the explained portion is subtracted to maintain consistency with the physical dictionary (Supplementary Methods 3). The number of iterative selection steps \(K\) (the pursuit depth) therefore provides a controlled residual-correction budget under noise and feature mismatch, and is shared between OMP and the learned solver for fair comparison.

Figure 4 closes the gap between Figs. 2 and 3 at the level of the solver itself. Figure 2 established that neighboring directions lie on a smooth physical manifold in the dictionary, whereas Fig. 3 showed that classical OMP fails when it collapses that broad local structure to a single greedy choice under speech-driven mismatch. The remaining question is therefore mechanistic: how does the learned solver convert the same broad physical evidence into a stable sparse update?

To make the solver steps visible on the angle axis, panels b-c plot angle-aggregated forms of the atom-level variables in Fig. 4a: the physical correlation \(g_t(\theta)\), the QK score \((q_tK_t^\top)(\theta)\), the routing weight \(w_t(\theta)=\sum_m w_{t,\theta,m}\), and the gated update \(\Delta x_t(\theta)=\|\eta\hat{\Delta x}_{t,\theta,\cdot}\|_2\). Panel b isolates the routing-formation branch directly on a shared 70° validation exemplar. The stage-0 physical correlation \(g_t(\theta)\) remains broad over nearby directions, the learned QK score \((q_tK_t^\top)(\theta)\) retains structure on that same manifold, and the routing weight \(w_t(\theta)\) collapses it to a sharply local gate. The network therefore does not replace the physics term; it transforms diffuse physical ambiguity into a localized routing decision using learned manifold context.

Panel c then closes the update loop on that same exemplar. Applying the gate produces a gated update \(\Delta x_t(\theta)\) that is far more localized than the raw physical correlation \(g_t(\theta)\), after which the first dictionary-consistent update changes the correlation landscape from \(g_t = D^\top r_t\) to \(g_{t+1} = D^\top r_{t+1}\). The lower summaries show that this is not a single-example curiosity: across the validation set, the cumulative local mass of \(\Delta x_t\) rises almost entirely within a narrow 0-15° neighborhood, and the residual norm drops across the unrolled stages. The solver therefore performs staged dictionary-consistent correction rather than a one-shot classification shortcut.

Panel d then makes the bridge back to Fig. 4a explicit. The upper block shows the mode-resolved routing tensor \(w_{t,\theta,m}\), and summing over the mode index \(m\) recovers the angle-level gate \(w_t(\theta)\) plotted in panel b. The lower block shows the mode-resolved gated-update magnitude \(|\eta\hat{\Delta x}_{t,\theta,m}|\), and taking its per-angle \(\ell_2\) norm recovers the angle-level update \(\Delta x_t(\theta)\) plotted in panel c. The angle-axis curves in panels b-c are therefore direct reductions of the solver's internal variables in panel a rather than detached diagnostics.

Panel e places that mechanism under ablation. Across the shared five-seed clean sweep, the guided solver is strongest, router-bypass degrades substantially, the OMP baseline remains intermediate, and dense routing collapses to near chance. Figure 4 therefore closes the sample-level mechanism: learned routing forms an angle-local gate from manifold-structured evidence, that gate maps coherently from mode-resolved internal variables to angle-axis updates, the gated update reduces the residual, and removing the routing logic removes most of the gain. The population-level consequences of that routing behavior and its robustness under noise are examined in Fig. 5.

When the learned weights concentrate on a single entry, the update reduces to classical greedy selection; when they spread across nearby angles, the solver exploits the smooth structure of the angle manifold to regularize its choices. The sparse-reconstruction viewpoint adopted here parallels classical sparse DOA methods that discretize a propagation manifold and solve for a sparse angular spectrum [@malioutov2005sparse_doa; @baraniuk2007compressive_sensing].

### Learned routing aligns with physical structure and resists noise (Fig. 5)
Having established in Fig. 4 how the solver sharpens, aggregates, and updates ambiguous sample-level correlations, we next ask whether that routing behavior scales to the full dataset and remains anchored to the physical manifold rather than learning an arbitrary pattern.

Figure 5 is organized in three steps. Panel a benchmarks four decoder families under noise; panel b gathers the full confusion structure of all four decoders into one unified family comparison; panel c then anchors the learned router to the physical angle manifold; panels d-e move from a representative local conditional-output view to the five-seed clean per-angle benchmark across the same four decoders.

Manifold-aligned routing confers noise robustness at the task level (Fig. 5a). On held-out speech recordings, the guided solver outperforms router-bypass, the OMP baseline, and dense routing across signal-to-noise ratio levels. To understand the mechanistic basis of this robustness, we next examine the learned router itself against both a physical anchor and a classical decoding reference.

The unified confusion-family panel (Fig. 5b) shows how these decoder families diverge across the full angle grid. The OMP baseline yields a broader and more fragmented confusion pattern, with only partial near-diagonal concentration and substantial local off-axis spillover. By contrast, the guided solver concentrates row-normalized probability sharply along the diagonal, indicating that it predicts the correct direction for each incidence angle with high consistency. Dense routing collapses probability mass toward a single preferred output mode, whereas router-bypass retains limited local structure but much broader leakage than the guided solver. Taken together, these four confusion maps provide the global context for the structure-alignment and local comparisons that follow.

The physical angle manifold exhibits characteristic correlations in the dictionary \(H\): nearby angles share spectral features, producing a banded correlation structure. Notably, the learned router's query-key (QK) correlation mirrors this physical structure with even sharper diagonal locality (Fig. 5c). The correlation between the upper-triangular entries of the dictionary correlation and QK correlation matrices is statistically significant, confirming that the router respects the geometry of the physical angle manifold rather than fitting an unconstrained attention pattern. This physical alignment is the core interpretability result.

Because the dense-routing local profiles are nearly degenerate, the most informative local view remains the guided-versus-router-bypass comparison: across four representative interior angles, the guided solver sharpens the conditional prediction profile around the correct angle and its immediate neighbors, whereas the router-bypass ablation shows broader off-axis leakage (Fig. 5d). A four-way per-angle benchmark (Fig. 5e) then places all decoders on the same angle grid using five-seed clean mean accuracy curves from the same experiment family summarized in Fig. 5a, rendered as a 3-angle centered moving-average display with light \(\pm 1\) s.e.m. shading: the guided solver remains strongest overall, the OMP baseline remains intermediate, router-bypass degrades substantially, and dense routing remains near chance across almost the entire angle set. Full ablation variants and band-resolved diagnostics are provided in Supplementary Methods 1-5.

### Physical encoding reproduces across materials, and material-frequency structure governs screening outcomes (Fig. 6)
The preceding analyses (Figs. 1-5) used a single acrylic plate as the sensing target. A natural question is whether the direction-dependent encoding phenomenon and its downstream utility are specific to one surface or reflect a more general consequence of dispersive wave superposition. We test this by applying an object-specific screening workflow to five structurally distinct everyday objects spanning a broad range of damping, stiffness, and geometric complexity: an acrylic plate, a paper cup, a wooden cutting board, a cardboard box, and a laptop shell (Fig. 6a). Each object undergoes independent white-noise calibration, transfer-function estimation, original-side basis learning, and matched downstream evaluation.

All five targets produce structured angle-frequency transfer functions (Fig. 6b), confirming that the spectral-filtering phenomenon established in Fig. 1 is not material-specific. Although their resonance patterns differ, every object exhibits a reproducible direction-dependent encoding over the same 0-180° angular grid. Fig. 6c then makes the low-rank continuity explicit as its own panel: applying the same row-wise mean-centered magnitude SVD used in Fig. 2 to each material-specific dictionary, the cumulative mode-coverage curves and rank90/rank95 summaries show that only a modest number of modes is needed to capture most encoder energy for each material, extending the low-rank physical-dictionary view from Fig. 2 beyond the single acrylic plate.

The cross-material comparison also reveals why these objects are not interchangeable. Fig. 6d closes the mechanism-to-task bridge directly. The left comparison shows that stronger overall response energy does not automatically imply better decoding, while the adjacent Top-1 and mean-absolute-error summaries show the matched screening outcome with uncertainty. Cardboard box still yields the strongest overall screening performance, whereas wooden board remains the strongest backup. Acrylic plate and paper cup remain competitive but weaker overall, and laptop shell performs worst. Fig. 6e then gives each target its own frequency-structure card: the upper trace shows where response magnitude is concentrated, the middle trace shows where directional variation is concentrated, and the lower trace projects the selected informative band back into angle space as a representative directional code. These cards make the material differences more legible: each object places its informative directional structure in a different frequency region and converts that structure into a different directional code, even though all remain low-dimensional. The cross-material comparison therefore supports a more precise interpretation: structured direction-dependent encoding is generic across everyday objects, low-dimensionality broadly reproduces across materials, and practical object choice is governed by how each material distributes directional information across frequency bands rather than by the existence of a fingerprint alone.

## Discussion
These results establish a complementary sensing paradigm in which passive everyday structures function as naturally randomized physical encoders for sound. A single, non-contact vibration readout contains enough information to decode DOA because the object converts incidence direction into a reproducible spectral fingerprint (Fig. 1), shifting the sensing footprint from an array aperture to a single optical spot. The paradigm aligns with the broader recognition that disorder and scattering in complex media can serve as a computational resource rather than an obstacle to sensing [@gigan2022imaging_computing_disorder; @rotter2017complex_media]. Prior implementations of this principle have relied on engineered disordered structures, such as randomized resonant metamaterials for compressive vibration identification in the elastic domain [@jiang2020randomized_metamaterial] and programmable coding metasurfaces for single-pixel compressive DOA estimation in the electromagnetic domain [@hoang2021single_pixel_doa]. We show that unmodified everyday objects already possess sufficient structural complexity to encode spatial information in the acoustic domain, without requiring purpose-built metamaterial designs.

The physical basis of the encoding is the direction-dependent superposition of dispersive structural modes. In this modal view, incidence direction changes the relative participation of a limited set of modes, yielding a low-rank angle-frequency response whose singular spectrum decays rapidly (Fig. 2a,b), consistent with eigenchannel interpretations of complex media [@popoff2010transmission_matrix; @mosk2012complex_media] and with analogous low-rank concentration observed in engineered randomized metamaterials [@jiang2020randomized_metamaterial]. Our results extend this physical-encoder view to natural, unmodified structures and show across five structurally distinct targets (Fig. 6) that direction-dependent transfer functions remain low-dimensional and task-relevant under the same centered-magnitude singular-spectrum criterion used for the acrylic plate in Fig. 2, rather than being confined to a single engineered panel. This interpretation depends on preserving the native structural response: because the optical readout does not mechanically load the target, the fingerprints can be attributed to the object itself rather than to a sensor-induced artifact [@ewins2000modal; @bi2013transducer_mass_loading]. Projecting into the dominant subspace improves robustness by retaining the physically informative variation while suppressing noise; however, as damping increases, modal overlap grows and directional identifiability can degrade [@ewins2000modal; @inman2013engineering_vibration].

That modal overlap, compounded by content-induced variation in speech, is what causes classical greedy pursuit to fail and spread spurious off-axis correlations (Fig. 3d-f). The physics-guided unrolled solver addresses this ambiguity by learning a soft routing distribution that regularizes selection using context across the entire angle manifold while preserving residual-consistent updates anchored to the physical dictionary (Fig. 4a). The approach embeds domain knowledge as structural inductive bias rather than learning from data alone, consistent with the broader framework of physics-informed machine learning [@karniadakis2021piml; @gregor2010lista; @monga2021unrolling]. Relative to non-negative matrix factorization (NMF)-based single-sensor localization and black-box deep neural network (DNN) classifiers, the key advantage is mechanistic interpretability: the learned routing aligns with the physical angle manifold (Fig. 5c), sharpens final-prediction structure relative to the classical and ablated decoders (Fig. 5b), and provides the inference backbone for the cross-material screening workflow after object-specific calibration (Fig. 6d; Supplementary Methods 1-5). The solver therefore succeeds by exploiting manifold smoothness already present in the dictionary, stabilizing inference under noise and nonstationary speech (Fig. 5a).

Beyond our specific implementation, the same array-free sensing idea has been explored in several modalities. El Badawy and Dokmanić showed that a single microphone embedded in an ad hoc LEGO scatterer can localize sound sources via NMF of the direction-dependent transfer function [@elbadawy2018lego_doa], whereas DiPassio et al. and subsequent work used contact-mounted piezoelectric sensing to decode structure-borne directional signatures from panels and beams [@dipassio2023doa_single_sensor; @rutowski2024reverb_single_sensor; @rutowski2025_structural_beamforming]. These modalities are complementary: contact sensors and embedded microphones offer compact form factors suited to integrated devices, whereas non-contact readout is preferable when preserving the encoder's physical integrity is essential or when the target cannot be instrumented.

Several constraints bound the present demonstration. The angular grid spans a half-plane (0-180°) with 5° resolution; extending to full azimuthal or elevation coverage will require additional calibration geometry and may encounter symmetry-related ambiguities. All experiments were conducted in a controlled acoustic environment with a single static source; real-world deployment would need to contend with reverberation, multiple simultaneous sources, and moving emitters. The LDV, while ensuring measurement fidelity, is a laboratory instrument that limits field deployment; future work could explore whether emerging compact laser vibrometers or high-bandwidth microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) alternatives retain sufficient sensitivity. Per-object calibration and matched screening are currently required (Fig. 6); whether transferable representations can reduce this overhead remains an open question. Even under corpus-internal held-out folds, generalization to a separately collected external speech corpus remains untested.

The central open frontier is whether invariances of the angle-frequency manifold can be identified across objects, enabling calibration-efficient or few-shot transfer. A second frontier is to quantify information-theoretic limits of single-point decoding as fingerprint separability degrades with increasing damping or angular density (Fig. 2a), and to determine how mounting conditions and LDV spot placement trade off robustness versus sensitivity. Extending the framework to multi-source and moving-source regimes will require modeling how fingerprints superpose and evolve across time windows. More broadly, the principle that passive structural complexity can serve as a computational resource for wave-field inference suggests applications beyond acoustics, including vibration-based structural health monitoring, seismic source localization, and compact sensing for internet-of-things (IoT) and assistive-technology devices where conventional arrays are impractical.

## Methods

### Experimental setup and data acquisition
All processing and learning were implemented in Python with PyTorch and trained on Apple Silicon (MPS). Unless otherwise stated, NumPy and PyTorch random seeds were fixed to 42.

Experiments used a single loudspeaker scanned over a half-plane angular grid comprising 37 incidence directions from 0° to 180° in 5° increments. Out-of-plane surface velocity was measured at a fixed LDV spot on each object while the mounting configuration was held fixed throughout calibration and evaluation for that object. All downstream processing used waveforms resampled to 16,000 Hz, and each clip was approximately 3 s long.

Two excitation regimes were used: (i) broadband white-noise playback for dictionary calibration and fingerprint repeatability diagnostics, and (ii) speech recordings for end-to-end DOA decoding and robustness experiments. The four frequency bands in Fig. 1e (0.3-0.5, 0.5-1, 1-2, and 2-3 kHz) tile the analysis band and were analyzed separately to probe frequency-dependent dispersive behavior. Five objects were selected to span a range of damping and structural complexity: acrylic plate, paper cup, wooden board, cardboard box, and laptop shell.

### Signal processing and feature extraction
The LDV measures out-of-plane surface velocity \(V(x_L,y_L,\omega)=i\omega W(x_L,y_L,\omega)\), where \(W\) is the displacement field and \((x_L, y_L)\) denotes the fixed laser measurement location. For incidence direction \(\theta\), the complex single-point response is \(Y(\omega;\theta)=V(x_L,y_L,\omega)\). For each recorded clip, a short-time Fourier transform (Hann window, 2,048 samples, hop 512) is computed and collapsed into a time-averaged power spectrum:

$$\widehat{S}(\omega_k;\theta)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_t |V[k,t]|^2, \qquad (3)$$

where \(V[k,t]\) is the complex STFT coefficient at frequency bin \(k\) and frame \(t\), and \(T\) is the number of retained frames. The spectrum is restricted to [300, 3,000] Hz (\(F = 346\) bins). Because the observed fingerprints are magnitude statistics, each clip is summarized by a log-power feature vector \(y[k]=\log_{10}(\widehat{S}+\epsilon)\), then standardized per frequency bin using white-noise calibration statistics to yield a normalized feature \(\tilde{y}[k]\) (Supplementary Methods 1). These normalized log-power fingerprints are the observables used for downstream inference. Accordingly, the sparse model in Results (Eq. 2) should be read as a feature-space approximation derived from the physical response in Eq. 1: the complex response \(Y(\omega;\theta)\) is converted into magnitude statistics, discretized over frequency and angle, and standardized before sparse matching. Equation 2 is therefore not a literal complex-domain superposition law and not a direct power-additivity statement.

### Physical dictionary formulation and subspace projection
The normalized features serve as the basis for constructing the angle-indexed dictionary used in inference. Per-angle prototypes are averaged across calibration trials to form columns \(h_e\), where \(e\) indexes one of the \(E\) candidate directions, yielding the dictionary \(H=[h_1,\dots,h_{37}]\in\mathbb{R}^{F\times E}\), with \(E=37\). This matrix \(H\) is the calibration-derived discrete angle-frequency dictionary corresponding to the continuous transfer function \(H(\theta, f)\) introduced in Results. Singular-value decomposition (SVD) of the mean-centered magnitude dictionary shows rapid early saturation (Fig. 2a). Six modes capture 80.3% of the centered-magnitude energy. Extending to eight modes raises the cumulative fraction to 85.1%.

For projected inference, both the test fingerprint and the calibration dictionary are mapped into this low-rank subspace: \(z=U_r^\top \tilde{y}\) and \(A=U_r^\top H\). This projection provides the compressed representation used in the sparse inverse problem introduced in Results (Eq. 2) and focuses inference on the physically significant channels while suppressing noise.

### Inference algorithms and network architecture
Using the sparse inverse problem introduced in Results (Eq. 2), DOA inference seeks a sparse coefficient vector over the projected dictionary \(A\). The formulation rests on three assumptions: (1) over each analysis window the target behaves as a linear time-invariant system, so direction-dependent responses superpose in the frequency domain; (2) \(Hx\) is a sparse prototype approximation in standardized fingerprint space, not a literal power-additivity law; and (3) SVD projection preserves angle-indexed structure while suppressing noise. The representative Kirchhoff-Love plate operator and Green's function derivation supporting assumption (1) are given in Supplementary Methods 4.

In the analytical orthogonal matching pursuit (OMP) baseline, the dictionary atom most correlated with the current residual is iteratively selected, added to the support set, and its contribution removed by least-squares refit and residual subtraction. After \(K=2\) stages, the predicted direction is \(\hat\theta=\theta_{\arg\max_e |x[e]|}\); the full recursive definition is given in Supplementary Methods 2. As shown in Results (Fig. 3d), OMP performs strongly on white noise but fails under speech content variation, motivating a learnable alternative.

The physics-guided solver unrolls the same \(K\)-stage pursuit into a differentiable network with learned attention-based routing. The residual is initialized as \(r_0 = z\) and the sparse coefficient vector as \(x_0 = 0\). At each stage \(t = 1,\dots,K\), the physical match score between the residual and every dictionary atom is computed as:

$$g_t = A^\top r_t, \qquad (4)$$

where \(r_t\) is the current residual and each entry of \(g_t\) quantifies how well a candidate direction explains the unexplained observation through the physical dictionary. Rather than selecting the single best-matching atom as in OMP, a transformer encoder (embedding dimension \(d_\mathrm{model}=128\), 2 attention heads, 1 encoder layer) uses the current residual to query the dictionary and produces soft routing weights via scaled dot-product attention:

$$s_t[e] = \frac{\langle q_t, k_e \rangle}{\sqrt{d_k}}, \qquad w_t = \mathrm{softmax}(s_t), \qquad (5)$$

where \(q_t\) is a learned query derived from the current residual, \(k_e\) is the learned key associated with dictionary atom \(e\), and \(d_k\) is the key dimension. In this setting, \(w_t\) is a manifold-aware routing distribution over neighboring angle hypotheses rather than a sequence-model token weight. These routing weights gate the physical match scores to produce a sparse update:

$$\Delta x_t = w_t \odot g_t, \qquad (6)$$

where \(\odot\) denotes element-wise multiplication. The sparse coefficient vector is accumulated as \(x_{t+1} = x_t + \eta\,\Delta x_t\), where \(\eta\) is a learned step-size parameter that scales each stage-wise sparse update before residual correction, and the residual is corrected by dictionary-consistent subtraction:

$$r_{t+1} = r_t - A\,\Delta x_t, \qquad (7)$$

ensuring that each stage explains a portion of the observation through the physical dictionary. After the final stage, expert-level logits are obtained by L2 aggregation of atom-level coefficients within each angular direction, yielding a probability vector:

$$p[e] = \frac{\lVert x_K^{(e)} \rVert_2}{\sum_{e'} \lVert x_K^{(e')} \rVert_2}, \qquad (8)$$

where \(x_K^{(e)}\) denotes the sub-vector of \(x_K\) corresponding to direction \(e \in \{1,\dots,E\}\). The predicted DOA is \(\hat\theta = \theta_{\arg\max_e\, p[e]}\). The routing weights \(w_t\) can be directly inspected as mechanistic evidence of manifold-aligned selection (Fig. 5c); Supplementary Methods 3 provides the complete per-stage equations.

The network is trained to minimize a composite loss combining three terms: a cross-entropy classification loss \(\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{cls}\) over the direction probabilities \(p[e]\), a reconstruction loss \(\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{rec} = \lVert r_K \rVert_2^2\) that penalizes unexplained residual energy and thereby enforces consistency with the physical dictionary, and a monotonicity regularizer \(\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{mono}\) that encourages sparse coefficients to concentrate on the selected direction across successive stages:

$$\mathcal{L} = \alpha\,\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{cls} + \beta\,\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{rec} + \gamma\,\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{mono}, \qquad (9)$$

with \(\alpha=1.0\), \(\beta=0.2\), and \(\gamma=0.5\). Optimization is performed with Adam (learning rate \(10^{-3}\), weight decay \(10^{-4}\)) for 20 epochs with batch size 32.

### Calibration protocol and outer-fold cross-validation
The dictionary \(H\) described above is constructed exclusively from white-noise calibration recordings, using all 3 clips per angle. The transfer function \(H(\theta,f)\) was estimated as the geometric-mean magnitude ratio of the LDV response to the source signal in the STFT domain, averaged over calibration trials. Calibration clips are never used for training or evaluation of the DOA decoder.

For speech decoding (Figs. 4-5), each angle contributes 260 speech clips (9,620 total). Clips are assigned deterministically to five outer folds by the rule \(\mathrm{fold} = \mathrm{clip\_id} \bmod 5\), producing 52 clips per angle per fold. For outer fold \(f\), fold \(f\) serves as the held-out test set, fold \((f+1) \bmod 5\) serves as the validation set, and the remaining three folds form the training set, yielding 5,772/1,924/1,924 train/validation/test samples in total. Model architecture, loss weights, optimizer, and the 20-epoch training schedule are fixed across folds; best-epoch selection uses only the validation fold. All speech results use the fixed \(H\) estimated from white-noise calibration only, and scalar performance metrics are reported from held-out outer-test predictions.

For cross-material screening experiments (Fig. 6), object-specific white-noise calibration data are acquired for each target and used to estimate the corresponding \(H\). For Fig. 6c, each material-specific dictionary is then analyzed with the same row-wise mean-centered magnitude SVD used for Fig. 2, that is, on \(|H| - \mathrm{mean}_{\theta}(|H|)\). For each object, an original-side basis is learned from the matched normalized reference clips, and downstream evaluation is performed on the corresponding normalized material-side clips with the same routed soft-OMP evaluator. These cross-material results are used to rank candidate objects under a matched screening workflow rather than to constitute a second five-fold speech benchmark. Universality is therefore assessed at the level of structured direction-dependent encoding plus a shared screening workflow, not by reusing weights across objects.

### Discriminability analysis
Before evaluating the learned solver, encoding quality is quantified independently of any inference algorithm (Fig. 3). White-noise and speech stimuli are compared using the same dictionary \(H\). For each stimulus type, within-angle Pearson correlation (mean pairwise \(\rho\) among clips at the same angle) and between-angle correlation (mean pairwise \(\rho\) across different angles) are computed. The discriminability margin is defined as within \(\bar{r}\) minus between \(\bar{r}\); a positive margin indicates that the encoding preserves directional information (Fig. 3c). Effect sizes are reported as Cohen's \(d\) and significance via the two-sided Mann-Whitney \(U\) test. Formal definitions are given in Supplementary Methods 5.

### Dose-response and noise robustness
For the dose-response analysis (Fig. 3f), noise levels are swept in two conditions: (i) white-noise signal with additive speech-spectrum noise, and (ii) speech signal with additive babble noise. For noise-robustness evaluation, zero-mean white noise is added at SNR levels \(\infty\), 30, 20, 15, 10, 5, and 0 dB in the time domain, using the same feature extraction and dictionary for each level.

### Solver ablations
The upstream clean-condition sweep behind Fig. 4e and the benchmark curves in Fig. 5a/e still records experiment-facing artifact keys. In that sweep, *No Transformer* maps to the paper-facing *router-bypass* family, *No Type Bias* is the active *guided solver* family, and *Fixed Heuristic*, *G-fixed*, and *G-teacher* are numerically identical soft-OMP variants collapsed into the paper-facing *OMP baseline*. *Dense Routing* remains the uniform-weight failure mode.

### Evaluation metrics and statistics
Top-1 accuracy is reported on the discrete angle grid. Angular error is the minimal angular difference \(\Delta(\hat\theta,\theta)=\min_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|\hat\theta-\theta+360k|\), and root-mean-square error (RMSE) = \(\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_i \Delta(\hat\theta_i,\theta_i)^2}\). Unless noted otherwise, scalar speech-decoder metrics are reported as mean ± s.d. across the five outer test folds. In Fig. 3f, the white-noise signal curve uses clip-level SEM within each synthetic SNR dataset, whereas the speech-plus-babble curve uses SEM across the five precomputed sweep runs. Mechanistic visualizations such as training curves, confusion matrices, and routing maps are shown for representative folds, whereas aggregate performance statements in the main text refer to held-out outer-fold summaries. When statistical hypothesis tests are used, the test, sidedness, and multiple-comparison correction are stated alongside the corresponding figure panel. Effect sizes are reported as Cohen's \(d\); distribution comparisons use the two-sided Mann-Whitney \(U\) test.
