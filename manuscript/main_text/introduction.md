# Introduction

Sound source localization underpins robotics, human-computer interaction, structural monitoring, and biomedical sensing. Most practical systems estimate direction of arrival (DOA) using microphone arrays and beamforming or subspace methods such as Capon, MUSIC, and ESPRIT [@capon1969_fkw; @schmidt1986_music; @roy1989_esprit]. These approaches require spatial sampling and sensor placement around the scene, which is impractical for remote or fragile targets where only a single, non-contact measurement is feasible. A cross-disciplinary opportunity is to rethink scattering not as noise to suppress, but as a structured code that can be decoded.

A key biological precedent is monaural spatial hearing. The outer ear shapes incoming sound in a direction-dependent way, creating spectral cues that the auditory system learns to interpret [@blauert1997_spatial_hearing; @batteau1967_pinna; @shaw1974_eardrum]. Individuals with unilateral hearing loss can adapt and localize using monaural spectral information alone, demonstrating that a single receiver can support spatial inference when a structured spectral mapping exists [@vanwanrooij2004_monaural; @hofman1998_relearning]. This motivates the idea that direction can be encoded into spectral structure by the medium itself.

Non-contact optical and vibrometry techniques show that remote measurements can capture rich acoustic information. Optical microphone systems and laser Doppler vibrometry (LDV) recover acoustic content from surface vibrations without placing a sensor at the source [@lai2023_optical_microphone; @lai2022_mask_vibration]. These advances indicate that single-point, remote measurements preserve meaningful spectral structure, but they do not by themselves provide a principled route to DOA inference or explain why a model should generalize across materials and environments.

Recent single-sensor localization methods attempt to encode direction using engineered scattering structures and then decode the resulting spectral signatures using matrix factorization or learning-based models [@elbadawy2018_lego; @jiang2022_engineered; @owlet2021_mobisys; @earcase2023_mobihoc]. These studies validate the principle that scattering can encode direction, but they depend on bespoke structures or embedded sensors and offer limited interpretability about when and why a model fails. This leaves a gap for non-contact, single-point localization that is physically grounded, interpretable, and deployable on naturally occurring surfaces.

Natural structures already provide rich modal responses that vary with geometry and material properties. Rather than fabricating scatterers, we leverage these inherent modes and treat them as a physically grounded dictionary of direction-dependent spectral templates. This reframes the hardware challenge into a modeling challenge: if the physics is explicit, the decoder can be interpretable, and the conditions under which it generalizes can be diagnosed.

Physics-informed learning and algorithm unrolling show that embedding physical structure and iterative solvers can improve generalization and transparency [@raissi2019_pinns; @monga2021_unrolling], while dictionary learning provides compact, parts-based spectral representations [@lee1999_nmf; @fevotte2009_is]. However, these ideas have not been unified into a DOA framework that uses a physical dictionary derived from natural structural modes and operates with a single remote measurement.

We therefore evaluate the method across materials and noise levels and include ablations that isolate the contribution of the physical dictionary and the learned decoder. These analyses show that the hybrid approach is necessary: physics alone is underdetermined under realistic noise, while data-only models struggle to generalize across surfaces with different modal signatures. The combined model provides both performance and interpretability, which are essential for cross-disciplinary adoption.

Here we introduce a physics-informed localization framework that treats a vibrating plate as a direction-dependent spectral encoder and couples a physics dictionary with a learned decoder to infer DOA from a single LDV measurement. The dictionary is constructed from structural modal responses, enabling interpretability and systematic ablations. This is a physics-inspired analogy, not a physiological model or an HRTF simulation. Our contributions are threefold: (i) a non-contact, single-point DOA formulation grounded in structural physics; (ii) a hybrid physics-learning decoder that leverages an explicit dictionary rather than a black-box mapping; and (iii) experimental evidence across materials and noise conditions, including ablations showing that physics-only or data-only baselines are insufficient. We first describe the physical model and decoder, then present experimental results and ablations, and finally discuss limitations and broader implications for remote acoustic sensing.
